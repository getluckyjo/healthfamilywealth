# Behaviour change, exercise and weight when quitting smoking and beer (research notes, as of 22 Oct 2026)

Context: a relapsed male smoker is quitting cigarettes and beer on Mon 26 Oct 2026. He is starting weight training three times a week plus a daily walk, wants to lose weight, and his wife is losing patience with him. Most sources are primary papers or their abstracts, retrieved through Europe PMC and publisher pages. Publication dates are given. Cochrane full texts returned HTTP 403, so Cochrane figures come from the official abstracts.

## 1. How long does habit formation take, and what helps habits stick?

### Takeaway
The "66 days" figure (Lally 2010) is a median with a very wide range (18–254 days). The newest synthesis (Singh et al., Dec 2024) puts median times at 59–66 days and mean times at 106–154 days, with individual times from 4 to 335 days. Exercise habits take months, not weeks. Habits are built by repeating a behaviour in a stable context, cued by a specific when-and-where plan. Morning timing and self-chosen behaviours form habits faster.

### Cited Findings
- **Lally et al. 2010** (*European Journal of Social Psychology* 40:998–1009). 96 volunteers each picked a daily eating, drinking or activity behaviour in the same context and reported automaticity daily for 84 days. — [UCL news, Aug 2009](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit); [DOI 10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)
- Lally: the median time to reach 95% of the automaticity plateau was 66 days, with a range of 18–254 days. A critique notes that this median comes only from the 39 participants whose data fitted the curve well. Because the study ran for only 84 days, the long-end values are model extrapolations. — [The Behavioral Scientist analysis](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit)
- **Lally interview, 21 Jan 2026.** She says 66 days "is not a general rule" and that "how long habit formation takes is highly variable". A specific plan for when and where to act makes action more likely than a vague intention, and knowledge alone rarely changes behaviour. — [University of Surrey, 21 Jan 2026](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)
- **Singh, Murphy, Maher & Smith 2024**, "Time to Form a Habit", *Healthcare* 12(23):2488, published 9 Dec 2024. The review covered 20 studies with 2,601 participants (mean ages 21.5–73.5). Behaviours included physical activity (8 studies), healthy diet (8) and flossing (3). Eleven studies were rated high risk of bias. — [DOI 10.3390/healthcare12232488](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488)
- Singh 2024 results:
  - Only 4 studies reported time to habit: median 59–66 days, mean 106–154 days, individual range 4–335 days.
  - Pooled habit-strength gain from before to after intervention: SMD 0.69 (95% CI 0.49–0.88).
  - Frequency, timing, type of habit, individual choice, affective judgements (enjoyment), behavioural regulation and "preparatory habits" significantly influenced habit strength.
  - Morning practices and self-selected habits generally formed stronger habits.
  - Authors' conclusion: habits "can start forming within about two months". — [Singh 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488)
- **Buyalskaya, Milkman, Duckworth, Camerer et al. 2023**, *PNAS*. Machine learning on more than 12 million gym check-ins and more than 40 million hospital hand-washing events found no "magic number". The gym habit typically took months to form; hand-washing took weeks. More habitual gym-goers responded less to an incentive to attend more. — [DOI 10.1073/pnas.2216115120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2216115120)

### Inferences
- He should expect the training habit (3 sessions a week, which is less frequent than daily) to need roughly 3–6+ months before it feels automatic. A daily walk at a fixed time and place, ideally in the morning, should become automatic sooner.
- "Habit stacking" means anchoring the walk to an existing routine, for example straight after the morning coffee or at the hour he used to smoke. It is a practical use of the context-cue and preparatory-habit findings. I found no trial that tested "habit stacking" by that name.
- Fixed gym days and times (e.g. Mon/Wed/Fri after work) give a stable context cue. Self-chosen, enjoyable exercises help, per the affective-judgement and individual-choice findings.

### Gaps
- I could not check the full Lally (2010) paper. Secondary sources widely report that missing a single day did not materially affect habit formation, so treat that claim as likely but unconfirmed here.
- No trials directly measure how long it takes to break or extinguish a smoking or drinking habit, as opposed to forming a new one.
- Singh 2024 rests on only 4 studies that reported time to habit, and most included studies were at high risk of bias.

## 2. Implementation intentions (if-then plans) and coping plans: effect sizes for smoking and drinking

### Takeaway
If-then plans have a robust medium effect on goal attainment overall (d≈0.65). For smoking cessation, McWilliams 2019 gives OR 1.70 (quit rate 10.7% vs 4.9%); Malaguti 2020 gives g = 0.31. For alcohol the effects are small (weekly drinking d = −0.14) and they do not reduce binge drinking. Generic "high-risk-situation coping skills" relapse-prevention programmes showed no long-term benefit in Cochrane. Personalised if-then plans are cheap and worth using, but they are an add-on to medication and support, not a replacement.

### Cited Findings
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006** (*Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38:69–119), meta-analysis of 94 independent tests from 63 reports: implementation intentions improved goal attainment with d = 0.65 (95% CI 0.6–0.7). — [as cited in Frontiers in Psychology 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)
- **Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer 2025**, "The when and how of planning", *European Review of Social Psychology* 36(1):162–194 (online 2024). This newer meta-analysis covers 642 independent tests and builds a taxonomy of plan cues and responses. I could not retrieve its headline effect size. — [KOPS repository](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905)
- **McWilliams, Bellhouse, Yorke, Lloyd & Armitage 2019**, *Health Psychology* (smoking cessation, 12 studies):
  - Overall: OR 1.70 (95% CI 1.32–2.20), quit rate 10.7% vs 4.9% in controls.
  - Implementation intentions alone: OR 5.69 (1.39–23.25), 14.3% vs 3.6%.
  - As part of a multi-component intervention: OR 1.67, 8.2% vs 5.8%.
  - Heterogeneity was substantial, and few studies had long-term biochemically verified abstinence. — [DOI 10.1037/hea0000768](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000768)
- **Malaguti et al. 2020**, *Drug and Alcohol Dependence* (21 RCTs). Effects on substance use: alcohol g = 0.31 (95% CI 0.21–0.42); tobacco g = 0.31 (0.12–0.50). The authors call these small. — [DOI 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108120](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2020.108120)
- **Cooke, McEwan & Norman**, *Drug and Alcohol Review*, online Sep 2022 (16 studies):
  - Weekly alcohol consumption: d = −0.14 (−0.24 to −0.03).
  - Heavy episodic drinking: no effect, d = −0.01 (−0.10 to 0.08).
  - Stronger effects in community samples (d = −0.38) than in university samples (−0.04).
  - Paper delivery (d = −0.26) beat online delivery (−0.04). Volitional help sheets (d = −0.34) beat free-form plans (−0.07).
  - Effects diminished over time. — [DOI 10.1111/dar.13553](https://doi.org/10.1111/dar.13553)
- **Armitage 2016**, *Health Psychology* RCT (N = 168 smokers, 89 men):
  - Smokers who formed if-then or when-then plans were significantly more likely to quit (χ²(1) = 8.86, p < .01).
  - The effect was mediated by reduced smoking-habit strength (95% CI 0.02–0.14).
  - Plan "certainty" (when-then vs if-then) made no difference.
  - Similar effects appeared for cigarettes per day, dependence and craving. — [DOI 10.1037/hea0000344](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000344)
- **Armitage 2007**, worksite RCT (N = 90). Implementation intentions increased quitting and "worked best for individuals who were more motivated to quit at baseline". — [DOI 10.1037/1076-8998.12.4.376](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-8998.12.4.376)
- **Moody, Poe & Bickel 2017** list effect sizes from smoking implementation-intention trials: Armitage 2007 d≈0.39; Armitage 2008 d≈0.70; Armitage & Arden 2008 d≈0.75; Armitage 2016 d = 0.41; average d≈0.56. In their own lab relapse analogue the effect was d = 0.32 and not significant. — [Moody et al. 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5657006)
- **Cochrane: relapse prevention interventions** (Livingstone-Banks et al., Oct 2019; 81 studies, 69,094 participants):
  - Behavioural interventions in abstainers that "teach people to recognise situations that are high risk for relapse along with strategies to cope with them" gave "no worthwhile benefit": assisted abstainers RR 0.98 (0.87–1.11; 11 studies, n = 5,523; moderate certainty); unaided abstainers RR 1.06 (0.96–1.16).
  - Extended varenicline did prevent relapse: RR 1.23 (1.08–1.41; moderate certainty).
  - NRT in unaided abstainers: RR 1.24 (1.04–1.47). — [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD003999.pub6](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD003999.pub6)
- **Dyadic planning RCT** (Buitenhuis, Tuinman & Hagedoorn, *Psychology & Health*, Dec 2019; 176 single-smoking couples):
  - Quit rates at 3 months were similar whether plans were made with the non-smoking partner (33%) or alone (30%).
  - Cigarettes per day fell by almost 50% in both groups.
  - Relationship satisfaction declined minimally in both groups.
  - Involving the partner in planning did not add benefit. — [DOI 10.1080/08870446.2019.1703983](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2019.1703983)

### Inferences
- Write 5–10 concrete if-then plans before 26 Oct, on paper, which beat online delivery in the alcohol meta-analysis. Target his specific cues. Examples:
  - "If I finish dinner, then I walk round the block."
  - "If I'm offered a beer at the golf club, then I say 'I'm off it, sparkling water please'."
  - "If I crave in the car, then I chew gum and call X."
- These are cheap and have modest, real effects. The Cochrane null for skills-based relapse prevention means plans should not replace stop-smoking medication or support.
- For drinking, if-then plans cut average weekly intake but not binge sessions. Avoiding high-risk settings (section 3) matters more for beer.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the effect size of the 642-test Sheeran 2025 meta-analysis.
- I found no meta-analysis isolating coping planning (plans for obstacles) for smoking cessation in adults; most coping-plan RCT evidence is for physical activity.
- Long-term (≥12 months) biochemically verified smoking outcomes for implementation intentions are sparse.

## 3. Cue and trigger management, craving duration, and the withdrawal timeline

### Takeaway
Withdrawal mood symptoms peak in week 1 and last about 2–4 weeks (Hughes 2007; NHS: worst in the first 3 days, average 3–4 weeks). Most relapse happens in the first 8 days. Alcohol causally weakens the ability to resist a cigarette, dose by dose. The NHS advises avoiding trigger places for the first 28 days. Formal "cue exposure therapy" has no consistent evidence. Craving smokers overestimate how long and how intensely an urge will last. "Urge surfing" changed what smokers did with urges rather than the urges themselves.

### Cited Findings
- **NHS guidance (current page, undated):**
  - Withdrawal can start "within a few hours" of the last cigarette and is usually strongest in the first week, "especially the first 3 days".
  - Symptoms last 3–4 weeks on average, sometimes a few months.
  - Advice: avoid places or situations that make you want to smoke for the first 28 days; "get moving" with a quick burst of activity; use deep breathing and sugar-free gum.
  - Reaching 28 days smoke-free makes you 5 times more likely to stay quit. — [NHS Better Health: managing withdrawal](https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/staying-smoke-free/managing-nicotine-withdrawal-symptoms/)
- **Hughes 2007**, "Effects of abstinence from tobacco: valid symptoms and time course", *Nicotine & Tobacco Research* (review of 120 studies, 1990–2004).
  - Valid withdrawal symptoms: anger, anxiety, depression, difficulty concentrating, impatience, insomnia, restlessness. They "peak within the first week and last 2–4 weeks".
  - Possible abstinence effects: constipation, cough, dizziness, increased dreaming, mouth ulcers.
  - The review did not evaluate craving or hunger. — [DOI 10.1080/14622200701188919](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200701188919)
- **Hughes 2007 companion review.** Significant withdrawal occurs in at least half of quitters. "Increases in depression after abstinence, but not other symptoms, prospectively predict relapse." — [DOI 10.1080/14622200701188927](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200701188927)
- **Hughes, Keely & Naud 2004**, *Addiction*. Among unaided quitters, "most relapse occurs in the first 8 days", and only 3–5% achieve 6–12 months of prolonged abstinence from a given attempt. Interventions "should focus on the first week". — [DOI 10.1111/j.1360-0443.2004.00540.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2004.00540.x)
- **Sayette et al. 2005**, *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors*.
  - High-urge smokers felt 45 seconds pass more slowly.
  - They predicted their urges would "climb steadily" over 45 minutes of abstinence, but smokers who actually waited showed no such rise.
  - So "craving smokers overpredict the duration and intensity of their own future smoking urges". — [DOI 10.1037/0893-164x.19.1.88](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-164x.19.1.88)
- **Urge surfing** (Bowen & Marlatt 2009, *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors*; N = 123 student smokers; cue-exposure paradigm). Brief mindfulness "urge surfing" instructions did not reduce urges, but participants "smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over a 7-day follow-up" than controls. The authors suggest mindfulness changes the response to urges rather than the urges themselves. — [DOI 10.1037/a0017127](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017127)
- **Cue exposure therapy** (Conklin & Tiffany 2002, *Addiction*; meta-analysis of 9 outcome studies): "no consistent evidence for the efficacy of cue-exposure treatment as currently implemented". — [DOI 10.1046/j.1360-0443.2002.00014.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1360-0443.2002.00014.x)
- **Diers, Brand & Antons, May 2026**, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* (ERP meta-analysis). People with addictive behaviours show heightened brain responses to addiction cues: late positive potential vs controls g = 0.955; target vs control cues within addiction groups g = 0.633. This confirms that cues carry a strong pull. — [DOI 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106737](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106737)
- **Alcohol as a lapse trigger** (Kahler et al. 2014, *Psychopharmacology*; within-subject lab study, N = 100 heavy drinkers smoking 10–30 cigarettes/day).
  - Compared with placebo, smokers lit up 3.35 minutes earlier after low-dose alcohol (p = .08) and 6.36 minutes earlier after high-dose alcohol (0.8 g/kg; p = .0006).
  - The effect was partly mediated by stronger urges: "alcohol can reduce the ability to resist smoking in a dose-dependent fashion". — [DOI 10.1007/s00213-014-3613-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-014-3613-3)
- McKee et al. 2006: after an alcohol priming drink, smokers were less able to resist the first cigarette, started sooner and smoked more. — [DOI 10.1007/s00213-006-0551-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-006-0551-8)
- **Habit discontinuity** (Thomas, Poortinga & Sautkina 2016, *PLoS One*; N = 18,053, UK). After moving home, people's values guided their travel behaviour more strongly, and this effect decayed as time since the move increased. This supports a "window of opportunity" when contexts change. — [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0153490](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0153490)
- Haggar et al. 2025 found no evidence that New Year acts as a habit discontinuity for green purchasing, although sales of NRT and weight-loss products did peak in January. — [DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1550091](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1550091)
- **smokefree.gov** craving tactics: switch activity immediately, walk, jog or climb stairs, breathe deeply, go to places where smoking isn't allowed, and use short-acting NRT on top of the patch. It says cravings "don't last forever". — [smokefree.gov: how to manage cravings](https://smokefree.gov/challenges-when-quitting/cravings-triggers/how-manage-cravings)

### Inferences
- **Weeks 1–4** (26 Oct to about 23 Nov 2026) are the danger zone, and the first 8 days most of all. Plan avoidance of bars, the 19th hole and post-round beers, and golf partners who smoke, for at least the first 28 days.
- Because alcohol lowers resistance to the first cigarette in a dose-dependent way, quitting beer at the same time removes a major smoking-lapse trigger.
- When he returns to golf, script the round in advance. Examples: leave straight after the 18th, carry gum and water, and agree a non-drinking ritual with playing partners.
- Treat each craving as a wave that will peak and pass. Sayette shows his brain will overestimate how long it lasts. A 10-minute walk or brisk stairs is the default response (section 6).
- Withdrawal low mood predicts relapse. Persistent low mood after week 2 is a signal to seek help or medication, not to tough it out.

### Gaps
- The popular claim that "cravings last 3–5 minutes" appears in quit-line materials. Other lay sources say 10–20 minutes. I found no primary study that measures the duration of a single craving episode, so treat any specific number as a rule of thumb.
- I found no trials of deliberately avoiding cue-rich environments (as opposed to clinical advice). The NHS "28 days" advice is guidance, not trial-derived.
- I found no studies specific to golf courses or golf clubs.

## 4. Slips vs relapse: the abstinence violation effect, and self-compassion vs self-criticism

### Takeaway
Marlatt's abstinence violation effect predicts that guilt and self-blame after a slip turn it into full relapse. Real-time studies of smokers only partly support this. What predicts progression from slip to relapse is feeling like giving up and losing self-efficacy, not guilt as such. Quick "restorative coping" after a slip reduces further lapses that day. Self-compassion is consistently linked to better health behaviour and lower stress. Direct trial evidence in smoking is small but supportive.

### Cited Findings
- **Shiffman et al. 1996**, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* (N = 133; palm-top diaries recorded the first lapse within minutes).
  - Indicators of the abstinence violation effect (self-efficacy, attributions, affect) "generally failed to predict progression to relapse".
  - Smokers "who felt like giving up after the first lapse progressed more rapidly to a second lapse".
  - Those who "attempted restorative coping were less likely to progress to another lapse on the same day".
  - Stress-triggered lapses progressed faster. Lapses during eating or drinking, or with alcohol, progressed more slowly. — [DOI 10.1037//0022-006x.64.5.993](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-006x.64.5.993)
- **Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto 2011**, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology* (N = 203 smokers, 1,001 lapses recorded in near real time).
  - Responses to the first lapse did not predict relapse.
  - On later lapses, drops in self-efficacy accelerated progression to the next lapse (HR 1.09, 1.02–1.15).
  - Internal attributions of blame were slightly protective (HR 0.98, 0.97–0.99).
  - The nicotine patch slowed lapse progression (HR 0.58, 0.48–0.70), but high post-lapse guilt eroded this benefit (HR 1.08). — [DOI 10.1037/a0024451](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024451)
- **Buitenhuis, Hagedoorn & Tuinman 2021**, *Psychology & Health* (169 couples; 21-day diaries from quit day). On days when the smoker's self-efficacy and the partner's confidence in him ("other-efficacy") were both higher than usual, the smoker was less likely to smoke. — [DOI 10.1080/08870446.2021.1978443](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2021.1978443)
- **Liu, Conner, Lainidi, Yu & O'Connor**, *Health Psychology Review*, Aug 2026 (19 studies, N = 9,660).
  - Self-compassion correlated with health-promoting behaviours (r = .23), lower health-risk behaviour (r = −.19) and much lower stress (r = −.51).
  - The links were partly mediated by stress. Correlational data. — [DOI 10.1080/17437199.2026.2701279](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2026.2701279)
- **Kelly, Zuroff, Foa & Gilbert**, *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology* 29:727–755 (2010). In an RCT of self-compassionate imagery and self-talk used at urges, self-compassion training reduced smoking faster in participants low in readiness to change or high in self-criticism. Details come from secondary summaries; I did not see the full text. — [UNC self-compassion reference list](https://cls.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3019/2016/05/ReferenceList_CLS_SelfCompassion_Sept19.16.pdf); [Mindfulness Association summary](https://www.mindfulnessassociation.net/research-blogs/science-based-evidence-for-compassionate-imagery/)
- **Adams & Leary 2007**, *Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology*. A self-compassion induction after an "off-diet" food preload reduced distress and reduced later eating among highly restrictive eaters. This is the classic experiment against the "what-the-hell" effect. — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/782823)
- Withdrawal-induced depression prospectively predicts relapse (Hughes 2007). — [DOI 10.1080/14622200701188927](https://doi.org/10.1080/14622200701188927)

### Inferences
- **Pre-agreed slip protocol.**
  - A slip is data, not a verdict.
  - Within the hour: bin the rest of the pack, log the trigger, do a restorative action (walk, call a supporter), recommit, and keep using NRT or medication.
  - Avoid "I've blown it" thinking. Feeling like giving up is the specific predictor of relapse.
- Because he has relapsed before, his self-efficacy is fragile. Small visible wins help rebuild it, such as a day count and gym sessions logged.
- Self-compassion is not letting himself off. It means treating a slip with the problem-solving stance he would take with a friend, which the evidence links to lower stress and better follow-through.

### Gaps
- I found no large RCT of self-compassion training with verified long-term smoking abstinence. The evidence is mainly correlational plus small experiments.
- I did not find a recent meta-analysis that tests the abstinence violation effect itself in smokers.

## 5. Spouse/partner support, partner behaviour and relationship dynamics

### Takeaway
What partners do matters more than formal "partner support" programmes. In older couples, a man whose partner also quit was far more likely to quit: 48% vs 8% when the partner kept smoking (OR 11.8). The balance of positive to negative partner behaviours predicts abstinence; pressure and nagging provoke reactance. Cochrane finds that interventions designed to boost partner support do not improve quitting, largely because they fail to change partner behaviour.

### Cited Findings
- **Jackson, Steptoe & Wardle 2015**, *JAMA Internal Medicine* (English Longitudinal Study of Ageing; 3,722 married or cohabiting couples aged 50 and over).
  - Smoking: when one partner quit ("newly healthy"), men quit 48% vs 8% if the partner kept smoking (adjusted OR 11.82, 95% CI 4.84–28.90). Women: 50% vs 8% (OR 11.23, 4.58–27.52).
  - Physical activity: men 67% vs 26% (OR 5.28). Weight loss of 5% or more: men 26% vs 10% (OR 3.05).
  - A newly healthy partner beat a consistently healthy one (smoking, men OR 3.08, 1.43–6.62; women OR 5.45).
  - Conclusion: people are "more likely to make a positive health behavior change if their partner does too". — [DOI 10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.7554](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2014.7554)
- **Cohen & Lichtenstein 1990**, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* (N = 221 quitters; Partner Interaction Questionnaire with 10 positive and 10 negative behaviours, at 1 month). "The ratio of received positive/negative behaviors was a consistently better predictor of abstinence" than either alone, with higher ratios associated with abstinence. Partners did more positive things than quitters expected. — [DOI 10.1037//0022-006x.58.3.304](https://doi.org/10.1037//0022-006x.58.3.304)
- **Cochrane: enhancing partner support** (Faseru et al., Aug 2018; 14 RCTs, n = 3,370).
  - Abstinence RR 0.97 (0.83–1.14) at 6–9 months and 1.04 (0.88–1.22) at 12 months or more. Low-quality evidence.
  - Only 2 of 11 studies that measured partner support showed it actually increased.
  - Conclusion: "interventions that aim to enhance partner support appear to have no impact" on long-term abstinence, but most never achieved their aim of increasing support. — [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD002928.pub4](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002928.pub4)
- **Küng et al.**, *British Journal of Health Psychology*, May 2026 (two 21-day diary studies, including 71 adults quitting smoking with carbon-monoxide-verified abstinence).
  - Daily pressure from a close other was linked to more reactance-type (oppositional) behaviour. "Daily pressure appears consistently counterproductive."
  - Pressure was not related to smoking abstinence. Gentle persuasion "might be more effective… when coming from a romantic partner" (exploratory). — [DOI 10.1111/bjhp.70082](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.70082)
- **Umberson, Donnelly & Pollitt 2018**, *Journal of Health and Social Behavior* (838 spouses). Spouses monitor and regulate each other's health habits ("social control"). Health habits are "strong drivers of relationship dynamics". — [DOI 10.1177/0022146518790560](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022146518790560)
- **Meyler, Stimpson & Peek 2007**, *Social Science & Medicine* (systematic review of 103 articles). The research "overwhelmingly" shows couples are concordant in health behaviours as well as mental and physical health. — [DOI 10.1016/j.socscimed.2007.02.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2007.02.007)
- **Varenicline users, retrospective cohort** (*Tobacco Induced Diseases*, May 2023). Quit success was 39% with a non-smoking partner, 76% when the partner also stopped, and 31% when the partner kept smoking. These figures come from a search-result summary and were not checked against the full text. — [PMC10194048](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10194048/)
- **Dyadic planning RCT** (Buitenhuis 2019): making quit plans together with a non-smoking partner did not beat planning alone (33% vs 30% quit). Relationship satisfaction dipped minimally during the quit attempt in both groups. — [DOI 10.1080/08870446.2019.1703983](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2019.1703983)
- A partner's daily confidence in the quitter ("other-efficacy") was linked to lower same-day smoking risk. — [Buitenhuis 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2021.1978443)

### Inferences
- **For him:** his wife's frustration is predictable, given the social-control research and his previous relapse. Pressure tends to backfire, and her confidence in him tracks his day-to-day success. Rebuilding that confidence through visible actions, rather than promises, is the evidence-consistent route. Examples:
  - Booked gym sessions and walks she can see happen.
  - A shared day counter.
  - Removing cigarettes and beer from the house.
  - Telling her about slips himself, before she finds out.
- **For her:** specific positive behaviours (praise, practical help, a joint walk) and fewer negative ones (nagging, policing, sarcasm) matter more than overall support, per the ratio finding. Inviting her to join the daily walk or the alcohol-free period uses the "newly healthy partner" effect from Jackson 2015, applied to exercise and drinking.
- Formal partner-support programmes have not worked in trials. Simple, behavioural agreements are more defensible than a structured "support" curriculum.

### Gaps
- I found no studies of "show, don't promise" or trust repair after relapse in addiction. This is a reasonable inference from the social-control, other-efficacy and support-ratio studies, not a tested intervention.
- I found no research on whether relationship quality recovers after sustained abstinence in smokers specifically. Most couples research on relationship change after addiction treatment concerns alcohol and drugs, which are outside this scope.
- Jackson 2015 is observational and in adults aged 50 and over. Couples who change together may differ in unmeasured ways.

## 6. Exercise as a craving aid: smoking and alcohol

### Takeaway
A single bout of moderate-intensity exercise reliably and substantially cuts cigarette cravings: effect sizes of about SMD −1.9 to −2.0 in individual-participant-data meta-analyses, lasting up to about 30–50 minutes. A brisk 10–15 minute walk is the practical tool. For long-term abstinence, Cochrane (2019) found no proven benefit (RR 1.08, low certainty). A newer 2026 meta-analysis of 59 RCTs finds a modest benefit (continuous abstinence RR 1.15). Early evidence (2026) suggests acute exercise also reduces alcohol craving (g≈0.55).

### Cited Findings
- **Taylor, Ussher & Faulkner 2007**, *Addiction* (systematic review of 14 studies).
  - All 12 studies comparing a bout of exercise with a passive condition found reduced cravings, withdrawal symptoms or smoking.
  - Cravings fell rapidly during exercise and stayed reduced for up to 50 minutes afterwards.
  - Effects appeared at intensities from 60–85% of heart-rate reserve for 30–40 minutes down to 24% for 15 minutes, and with 5 minutes of isometric exercise.
  - Nearly all studies used temporarily abstaining smokers. — [Plymouth research portal record](https://researchportal.plymouth.ac.uk/en/publications/the-acute-effects-of-exercise-on-cigarette-cravings-withdrawal-sy-2/)
- **Haasova et al.**, *Addiction* (online Nov 2012, print 2013; individual participant data meta-analysis).
  - Physical activity vs control: strength of desire to smoke SMD −1.91 (−2.59 to −1.22; 15 studies); desire to smoke SMD −2.03 (−2.60 to −1.46; 17 studies).
  - "There is strong evidence that physical activity acutely reduces cigarette craving." — [DOI 10.1111/j.1360-0443.2012.04034.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2012.04034.x)
- **Haasova et al. 2014**, *Psychopharmacology* (individual data, n = 930, 19 RCTs).
  - Craving reduction vs control on a 0–100% scale: light activity −9.22, moderate −34.57, vigorous −31.29.
  - No demographic or smoking characteristic moderated the effect. "Moderate intensity PA could be recommended to all smokers." — [DOI 10.1007/s00213-014-3450-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-014-3450-4)
- **Cochrane: exercise interventions for smoking cessation** (Ussher, Faulkner, Angus, Hartmann-Boyce & Taylor, 30 Oct 2019; 24 trials, 7,279 participants).
  - Exercise plus cessation support vs support alone: RR 1.08 (0.96–1.22; 21 studies, n = 6,607; I² = 0%; low certainty).
  - No subgroup differences by exercise type; only 1 study tested resistance training alone.
  - In relapse prevention: RR 0.98 (0.65–1.47).
  - Conclusion: "no evidence that adding exercise… improves abstinence", but the evidence is insufficient to rule out a modest benefit. I found no newer Cochrane update. — [DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD002295.pub6](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD002295.pub6)
- **NEW: Singh, Petersen, Miatke et al.**, "Exercise-based interventions for smoking cessation", *Journal of Sport and Health Science*, online 7 Apr 2026 (59 RCTs, n = 9,083; searched to Mar 2025).
  - Exercise training increased continuous abstinence (RR 1.15, 1.01–1.32; 23 RCTs, n = 5,512) and 7-day point-prevalence abstinence (RR 1.21, 1.03–1.43; 18 RCTs, n = 4,455).
  - It reduced cigarettes per day (mean difference −2.12, −3.30 to −0.93).
  - Single bouts reduced cravings "immediately and up to 30 min post-exercise" (SMD −0.88 to −0.52), but not longer-term cravings.
  - Higher-intensity bouts gave larger craving reductions. Aerobic training improved continuous abstinence.
  - Evidence for resistance training and yoga was "more limited but suggested potential benefits". — [DOI 10.1016/j.jshs.2026.101138](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2026.101138)
- **Xu et al.**, *Frontiers in Public Health*, 18 Feb 2025 (47 studies, n = 4,267).
  - Acute exercise reduced desire to smoke (SMD −0.97, −1.40 to −0.54), strength of desire (SMD −1.75, −2.41 to −1.08) and irritability (SMD −0.61).
  - No long-term abstinence benefit: 7-day abstinence RR 1.17 (0.98–1.40); continuous RR 1.01 (0.88–1.17).
  - **Caution:** the abstract's results contradict its own text on direction, and several values are mislabelled. — [PMC11877127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11877127/)
- **Zhou et al.**, *Frontiers in Physiology*, Aug 2023. Acute exercise reduced cravings (SMD −1.64, −2.22 to −1.05) and most withdrawal symptoms, and improved positive mood (SMD 0.36). There was no significant effect on quit rates. — [DOI 10.3389/fphys.2023.1221898](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2023.1221898)
- **Resistance training pilot** (Ciccolo et al. 2011, *Nicotine & Tobacco Research*; n = 25; nicotine patch plus brief counselling in both arms).
  - 7-day abstinence at 3 months: 46% with resistance training vs 17% with contact control. At 6 months: 38% vs 17%. Prolonged abstinence: 16% vs 8% and 15% vs 8%.
  - Weight −0.6 kg vs +0.6 kg; body fat −0.5% vs +0.6%. — [DOI 10.1093/ntr/ntr068](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntr068)
- The full "Strength To Quit" RCT (n = 206; 12 weeks of resistance training, twice weekly) was designed in 2014. I did not retrieve its results. — [design paper, DOI 10.1016/j.mhpa.2014.05.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mhpa.2014.05.004)
- **Alcohol, systematic review** (Sampedro-Piquero et al., *European Journal of Sport Science*, Oct 2026; 6 studies, ages 18–29 with problematic use). A single exercise bout gave a moderate, significant reduction in alcohol craving (g≈0.55), often alongside less anxiety and negative affect. — [DOI 10.1002/ejsc.70258](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.70258)
- **Alcohol, RCT** (Gavigan, Prapavessis & Chavarria, *Addictive Behaviors*, Mar 2026; 68 heavy social drinkers aged 19–65).
  - Participants drank an alcohol "prime", then did 20 minutes of moderate treadmill walking or colouring.
  - Walking cut craving and wanting more during the activity (Bayes factor > 18). By the end, between-group differences were minimal.
  - The walking group drank less of the alcohol placebo relative to water. — [DOI 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108684](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108684)
- **Guidance:** NHS advises "a quick burst of activity" for cravings. Smokefree.gov advises a walk, jog or a few flights of stairs. — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/staying-smoke-free/managing-nicotine-withdrawal-symptoms/); [smokefree.gov](https://smokefree.gov/challenges-when-quitting/cravings-triggers/how-manage-cravings)

### Inferences
- **Craving first aid:** a 10–15 minute brisk walk at moderate intensity (able to talk, not sing). It works fast and lasts about 30–50 minutes. Schedule the daily walk for his highest-risk time of day, such as after dinner or the old beer hour, so it doubles as a pre-emptive craving buffer.
- Weight training three times a week is good for weight and body composition (section 7). Its specific effect on staying quit is plausible but under-tested. It should complement, not replace, medication and behavioural support.
- Exercise should not be "sold" to him as what keeps him off cigarettes long term. Its strongest proven role is short-term craving and mood relief, plus weight control.

### Gaps
- I found no large, adequately powered RCT of resistance training for cessation; Strength To Quit results were not retrieved.
- Long-term exercise-for-abstinence estimates disagree: Cochrane 2019 (null, low certainty) vs Singh 2026 (modest benefit). The 2026 review is not Cochrane, and I could not appraise its risk-of-bias handling beyond the abstract.
- The alcohol-craving evidence is young (6 small studies, mainly young adults), and I found no trial showing exercise improves long-term alcohol abstinence in heavy beer drinkers.

## 7. Weight gain after quitting: size, what limits it, resistance training, and net cardiovascular benefit

### Takeaway
Untreated quitters gain about 4.7 kg on average by 12 months, most of it in the first 3 months, with huge variation: 16% lose weight and 13% gain more than 10 kg. No weight-control strategy has moderate-certainty evidence of a lasting effect. The best signals are:
- personalised weight-management support, which helps short-term;
- exercise, about −2.1 kg at 12 months (low certainty);
- NRT and bupropion, which delay gain.

Generic diet education may harm quit rates. Even with weight gain, quitting roughly halves cardiovascular risk.

### Cited Findings
- **Aubin, Farley, Lycett, Lahmek & Aveyard 2012**, *BMJ* (meta-analysis of 62 studies).
  - Mean weight gain in untreated quitters: 1.12 kg at 1 month, 2.26 kg at 2 months, 2.85 kg at 3 months, 4.23 kg at 6 months and 4.67 kg (95% CI 3.96–5.38) at 12 months.
  - At 12 months: 16% lost weight, 37% gained under 5 kg, 34% gained 5–10 kg and 13% gained more than 10 kg.
  - Gains were similar across pharmacotherapies, and "most weight gain occurs within three months". — [PubMed 22782848](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22782848/)
- **Cochrane: interventions for preventing weight gain after smoking cessation** (Hartmann-Boyce, Theodoulou, Farley, Hajek … Aveyard, Oct 2021; CD006219.pub4). Part 1 tested interventions that target weight directly (37 studies):
  - **Personalised weight-management support:** −1.11 kg at end of treatment (−1.93 to −0.29; very low certainty). No evidence of effect at 12 months (−0.44 kg, −2.34 to 1.46).
  - **Detailed weight education without personalised assessment, planning and feedback:** no weight effect (12 months −0.21 kg) and "may have reduced smoking cessation rates" (RR 0.66, 0.48–0.90; 2 studies, n = 522).
  - **Intermittent very-low-calorie diet with full meal replacement and dietitian support:** −3.70 kg at end of treatment, but not significant at 12 months (−1.30 kg). Abstinence RR 1.73 (1.10–2.73; 1 study). In a second study, no one completed the diet.
  - **Weight-gain acceptance interventions:** quit rates up at 6 months (RR 1.42, 1.03–1.96) but not significant at 12 months (RR 1.25). — [PubMed 34611902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34611902/)
- **Same Cochrane review, Part 2** (cessation aids that may affect weight):
  - **Exercise:** −0.25 kg at end of treatment (not significant) but −2.07 kg at 12 months (−3.78 to −0.36; 3 studies, n = 182; low certainty).
  - **Bupropion:** −1.01 kg at end of treatment (−1.35 to −0.67); −0.26 kg at 12 months (not significant).
  - **NRT:** −0.52 kg at end of treatment (moderate certainty); −0.37 kg at 12 months (imprecise).
  - **Varenicline:** −0.23 kg at end of treatment (high certainty); +1.05 kg at 12 months (low certainty, imprecise). — [PubMed 34611902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34611902/)
- **Cochrane authors' conclusions:** "no intervention for which there is moderate certainty of a clinically useful effect on long-term weight gain". Also "no moderate- or high-certainty evidence that interventions designed to limit weight gain reduce the chances of people achieving abstinence". — [PubMed 34611902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34611902/)
- **Spring et al. 2009**, *Addiction* (10 RCTs). Combined smoking and behavioural weight-control treatment vs smoking treatment alone:
  - Short term (under 3 months): abstinence OR 1.29 (1.01–1.64); weight g = −0.30 (−0.57 to −0.02).
  - Long term (over 6 months): abstinence OR 1.23 (0.85–1.79); weight g = −0.17. Neither significant.
  - "No evidence that combining… produces any harm." — [DOI 10.1111/j.1360-0443.2009.02610.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2009.02610.x)
- **Contrasting older trial** (Hall et al. 1992, *American Journal of Public Health*; n = 158). Both weight-gain-prevention arms had higher smoking relapse than standard treatment. The authors suggest the interventions were too complicated and that "caloric restriction may increase the reinforcing value of nicotine". — [DOI 10.2105/ajph.82.6.799](https://doi.org/10.2105/ajph.82.6.799)
- **Clair et al. 2013**, *JAMA* (Framingham Offspring Study; 3,251 participants; mean follow-up 25 years).
  - Recent quitters without diabetes gained a median 2.7 kg over 4 years.
  - CVD vs continuing smokers: recent quitters HR 0.47 (0.23–0.94); long-term quitters HR 0.46 (0.34–0.63). These changed minimally after adjusting for weight change.
  - Conclusion: this "supports a net cardiovascular benefit of smoking cessation, despite subsequent weight gain". — [DOI 10.1001/jama.2013.1644](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2013.1644)
- **Resistance training** (pilot, n = 25): weight −0.6 kg vs +0.6 kg and body fat −0.5% vs +0.6% over the programme, compared with contact control. — [Ciccolo 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntr068)
- **Dulaglutide (GLP-1 drug) trial** (Lengsfeld et al., *eClinicalMedicine*, Feb 2023; n = 255, all on varenicline plus counselling).
  - 12-week abstinence: 63% with dulaglutide vs 65% with placebo (no difference).
  - Weight: −1.0 kg vs +1.9 kg; adjusted difference −2.9 kg (−3.59 to −2.3).
  - Gastrointestinal symptoms: 90% vs 81%. — [DOI 10.1016/j.eclinm.2023.101865](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2023.101865)
- **Dulaglutide 12-month follow-up** (Lüthi et al., *eClinicalMedicine*, Feb 2024). Abstinence at 52 weeks was 32% in both groups. Weight at 52 weeks was +2.8 kg vs +3.1 kg (adjusted difference −0.35 kg, not significant). Three months of treatment had no sustained effect on weight. — [DOI 10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102429](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102429)
- **Dulaglutide, sex differences** (Baur et al., *BMJ Nutrition, Prevention & Health*, Dec 2023). Weight gain of more than 6%: men 0% on dulaglutide vs 5% on placebo (not significant); women 1% vs 24%. — [DOI 10.1136/bmjnph-2023-000781](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjnph-2023-000781)
- **Dulaglutide, blood pressure** (Beck et al., *European Journal of Preventive Cardiology*, Oct 2025). Across the trial, systolic blood pressure stayed stable over 52 weeks despite a median weight gain of +3 kg. Blood pressure fell in those who gained 3 kg or less (−4.6 mmHg). — [DOI 10.1093/eurjpc/zwaf055](https://doi.org/10.1093/eurjpc/zwaf055)

### Inferences
- **Expectations:** budget for about 2–3 kg by Christmas 2026 and about 4–5 kg by October 2027 if he does nothing. That is the untreated average; his beer cut should offset part of it (see the second point below).
  - Frame the first 3 months as "quit first, maintain weight". Aggressive dieting can follow once he is past about 3 months smoke-free. This is consistent with Hall 1992, with Cochrane's signal that generic diet education may lower quit rates, and with the Spring meta-analysis showing no harm from structured support.
  - Personalised, structured support is reasonable from day one. It does not undermine quitting.
- Stopping beer removes substantial calories. For example, 10 pints a week is very roughly 1,800–2,000 kcal; this is an estimate not from the sources above. That alone may offset much of the expected gain. Checking alcohol's weight effect with the alcohol researcher is advisable.
- Weight training preserves and builds lean mass, so the scale may underestimate fat loss. Tracking waist circumference alongside weight is sensible.
- Cardiovascular risk falls by about half after quitting, even with typical weight gain. Weight gain is never a reason to go back to smoking.
- NRT and bupropion delay weight gain somewhat. GLP-1 drugs (dulaglutide, semaglutide) blunt gain while taken but have not improved quit rates. These are prescription choices for his GP.

### Gaps
- No RCT tested resistance training plus smoking cessation on 12-month weight in men. The only data are pilot-sized.
- No Cochrane update on weight gain after 2021 was found.
- No trial was found of GLP-1 drugs given for longer than 12 weeks after quitting, or of tirzepatide.

## 8. Health benefits timeline after quitting smoking

### Takeaway
Evidence-based milestones, per the US Surgeon General's 2020 report via the CDC:
- **Minutes to days:** heart rate falls and carbon monoxide normalises.
- **1–12 months:** cough and breathlessness improve.
- **1–2 years:** heart-attack risk drops sharply.
- **3–6 years:** excess coronary risk halves.
- **5–10 years:** excess mouth, throat and larynx cancer risk halves, and stroke risk falls.
- **10–15 years:** excess lung-cancer risk halves.
- **15 years:** coronary risk approaches that of a never-smoker.

Mental health also improves after quitting. Anxiety, depression and stress fall relative to continuing smokers.

### Cited Findings
- **CDC "Benefits of quitting"** (page dated 15 May 2024; primary source *Smoking Cessation: A Report of the Surgeon General*, 2020):
  - Minutes: heart rate drops.
  - 24 hours: nicotine in blood drops to zero.
  - Several days: carbon monoxide returns to non-smoker level.
  - 1–12 months: coughing and shortness of breath decrease.
  - 1–2 years: heart-attack risk drops sharply.
  - 3–6 years: added coronary heart disease risk cut by half.
  - 5–10 years: added risk of mouth, throat and voice-box cancer cut by half; stroke risk decreases.
  - 10–15 years: added lung-cancer risk cut by half; bladder, oesophagus and kidney cancer risks decrease.
  - 15 years: coronary heart disease risk close to a non-smoker's.
  - 20 years: mouth, throat, voice-box and pancreatic cancer risks close to a non-smoker's; added cervical cancer risk about halved.
  - Quitting can add up to 10 years of life expectancy. — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/about/benefits-of-quitting.html)
- **NHS:** reaching 28 days smoke-free makes you 5 times more likely to stay quit. — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/staying-smoke-free/managing-nicotine-withdrawal-symptoms/)
- **Taylor et al. 2014**, *BMJ* (26 longitudinal studies). Quitters vs continuing smokers:
  - Anxiety SMD −0.37 (−0.70 to −0.03), depression −0.25 (−0.37 to −0.12), stress −0.27 (−0.40 to −0.13).
  - Psychological quality of life +0.22 and positive affect +0.40.
  - Effects were similar in people with psychiatric or physical illness. — [DOI 10.1136/bmj.g1151](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g1151)
- **Clair 2013:** CVD hazard about halved in quitters, unaffected by weight gain. — [JAMA 2013](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2013.1644)

### Inferences
- **Motivating near-term milestones** for him: no carbon monoxide within days; withdrawal mood symptoms easing by weeks 2–4; the 28-day "5×" milestone (about 23 Nov 2026); breathing and cough better within months; heart-attack risk sharply lower by 2027–28.
- Mental-health improvement counters the common belief that smoking relieves stress. It can be shared with his wife as part of the "why".

### Gaps
- The widely circulated NHS and "quit app" hour-by-hour timelines (20 minutes pulse, 8 hours oxygen, 48 hours taste and smell, 72 hours breathing) were not retrieved from a primary source here. Several of these short-term claims are physiologically plausible but rarely sourced. Prefer the CDC/Surgeon General milestones above.
- The Cochrane 2021 update on mental health after quitting was not retrieved; Taylor 2014 BMJ was used.

## 9. Skin: smoking, alcohol, and daily sunscreen

### Takeaway
Smoking is strongly and consistently linked to facial ageing, including in identical twins. Heavy drinking (8 or more drinks a week) is linked to specific facial-ageing signs. Evidence that quitting reverses skin ageing is weak: one uncontrolled pilot. The strongest intervention evidence for skin is daily broad-spectrum sunscreen. In an RCT it stopped detectable photoageing over 4.5 years, with 24% less ageing than discretionary use.

### Cited Findings
- **Guyuron et al. 2009**, *Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery* (186 pairs of identical twins).
  - "The longer the twins smoked, the older they appeared" (p < 0.0001). Smokers had more wrinkles (p = 0.005) and more dark, patchy discolouration (p = 0.005).
  - Sun exposure, outdoor activities and lack of sunscreen were linked to older appearance. — [DOI 10.1097/prs.0b013e31819c4d42](https://doi.org/10.1097/prs.0b013e31819c4d42)
- **Goodman et al. 2019**, *Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology* (3,267 women aged 18–75; cross-sectional self-rating with photographic scales).
  - Smoking was linked to more forehead, crow's-feet, frown and lip lines, under-eye puffiness, nasolabial folds, oral commissures and reduced lip fullness.
  - Heavy alcohol use (8 or more drinks a week) was linked to more upper facial lines, under-eye puffiness, oral commissures, midface volume loss and visible blood vessels (p ≤ 0.042). — [PubMed 31531169](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31531169/)
- **Serri et al. 2010**, *Skinmed* ("Quitting smoking rejuvenates the skin"; pilot of 64 women in Milan; no control group). A proprietary algorithm estimated average skin "biological age" falling by about 13 years after 9 months of quitting, with significant changes by 6 months. The design is weak (uncontrolled, proprietary measure). — [PubMed 20839421](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20839421/)
- **Hughes, Williams, Baker & Green 2013**, *Annals of Internal Medicine* (RCT, Nambour, Australia; 903 adults under 55).
  - The daily broad-spectrum sunscreen group "showed no detectable increase in skin aging after 4.5 years".
  - Ageing was 24% less than with discretionary sunscreen use (relative odds 0.76, 95% CI 0.59–0.98).
  - β-carotene supplements had no overall effect. — [DOI 10.7326/0003-4819-158-11-201306040-00002](https://doi.org/10.7326/0003-4819-158-11-201306040-00002)

### Inferences
- **For a golfer**, daily sunscreen is the single best-evidenced skin intervention: face, neck, ears and backs of hands, reapplied on the course. It pairs naturally with the new routine and could be "stacked" onto teeth-brushing.
- Quitting stops further smoking-driven skin damage. Claims of dramatic reversal ("13 years younger") rest on very weak evidence and should not be promised.
- Cutting beer may reduce puffiness and visible facial blood vessels, by inference from the cross-sectional associations. Improvement after stopping has not been tested.

### Gaps
- No RCT or controlled cohort of skin change after quitting smoking was found.
- No study of skin change after reducing alcohol was found.
- Goodman 2019 included only women. Guyuron's twins were mixed-sex, but alcohol was not the focus.

## 10. What is genuinely new in 2024–2026

### Takeaway
New in 2024–2026:
- habit-formation timing (Singh 2024);
- a larger implementation-intention meta-analysis (Sheeran et al. 2025);
- a 59-RCT exercise-for-smoking meta-analysis showing modest abstinence gains (Singh et al. 2026);
- first trials of acute exercise for alcohol craving (2026);
- daily-diary evidence that partner pressure backfires (2026);
- a self-compassion and health-behaviour meta-analysis (2026);
- GLP-1 drug trials showing weight but not abstinence benefits (2023–2026).

The core Cochrane reviews (exercise 2019, weight gain 2021, partner support 2018, relapse prevention 2019) appear not to have been updated.

### Cited Findings
- **Habits:** Singh et al., *Healthcare*, Dec 2024. Median 59–66 days, mean 106–154 days, range 4–335 days; morning and self-chosen habits are stronger. — [DOI 10.3390/healthcare12232488](https://doi.org/10.3390/healthcare12232488)
- **Lally's own 2026 caution** that 66 days is "not a general rule". — [Surrey, Jan 2026](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)
- **Implementation intentions:** Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer, *European Review of Social Psychology* 2025 (642 tests). — [KOPS](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905)
- **Exercise and smoking:** Singh et al., *Journal of Sport and Health Science*, Apr 2026 (59 RCTs). Continuous abstinence RR 1.15; 7-day abstinence RR 1.21; craving relief up to 30 minutes. — [DOI 10.1016/j.jshs.2026.101138](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2026.101138)
- Also Xu et al., Feb 2025: acute craving relief, no long-term abstinence effect; internally inconsistent paper. — [PMC11877127](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11877127/)
- **Exercise and alcohol craving:**
  - Sampedro-Piquero et al., Oct 2026 (g≈0.55). — [DOI 10.1002/ejsc.70258](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.70258)
  - Gavigan et al., Mar 2026: a 20-minute walk reduced craving during activity in heavy social drinkers. — [DOI 10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108684](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108684)
- **Partners:** Küng et al., May 2026: daily pressure from close others is "consistently counterproductive" and provokes reactance. — [DOI 10.1111/bjhp.70082](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.70082)
- **Self-compassion:** Liu et al., *Health Psychology Review*, Aug 2026 (19 studies). r = .23 with health-promoting behaviour, r = −.19 with risk behaviour, r = −.51 with stress. — [DOI 10.1080/17437199.2026.2701279](https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2026.2701279)
- **GLP-1 drugs:**
  - Dulaglutide: weight −2.9 kg vs placebo at 12 weeks, no abstinence benefit, weight effect gone by 52 weeks. — [2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2023.101865); [2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2024.102429)
  - Dulaglutide also reduced alcohol intake by 29% vs placebo in the same smoking trial (relative effect 0.71, 0.52–0.97). — [Probst et al., JCI Insight 2023](https://doi.org/10.1172/jci.insight.170419)
  - Semaglutide phase 2a RCT (Hendershot et al., *JAMA Network Open*, May 2026; n = 24): no effect on primary lab smoking measures or cigarettes per day, but it reduced craving and body weight. — [DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.14898](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.14898)
  - Observational cohort in people with HIV (*AIDS*, Aug 2026; n = 123): starting semaglutide was followed by −2.5 cigarettes per day (95% CI −3.7 to −1.3). — [DOI 10.1097/qad.0000000000004596](https://doi.org/10.1097/qad.0000000000004596)
- **Cue reactivity:** ERP meta-analysis, May 2026, confirming heightened neural cue reactivity in addiction. — [DOI 10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106737](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2026.106737)

### Inferences
- The 2026 exercise meta-analysis slightly strengthens the case that a sustained exercise programme modestly helps abstinence. The robust, actionable finding is still acute craving relief.
- GLP-1 medicines are an emerging option for limiting weight gain after quitting, but not yet a quit aid, and benefits fade when treatment stops.

### Gaps
- I searched for, but did not find, 2024–2026 updates of the Cochrane reviews on exercise (CD002295), weight gain (CD006219), partner support (CD002928) or relapse prevention (CD003999). Absence from my searches is not proof that no update exists.
- Results of large ongoing GLP-1 smoking-cessation RCTs were not found.
