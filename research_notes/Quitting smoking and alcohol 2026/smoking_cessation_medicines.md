# Smoking cessation medicines and nicotine products: evidence as of October 2026

Context for the report writer: these notes support a man who relapsed to cigarettes and quits on Monday 26 Oct 2026. His plan is combination NRT (patch plus gum or lozenge), stepped down over 10–12 weeks and stopped by about Day 90. He asked: "Don't nicotine patches keep me addicted?" His country is unknown, so availability is noted by country. Research date: 22 Oct 2026.

Terms used below:
- OR = odds ratio; RR = risk ratio; CI = confidence interval; CrI = credibility interval (the Bayesian equivalent of a CI).
- NMA / cNMA = network meta-analysis / component network meta-analysis.
- "Quit rate" = abstinence at 6 months or longer, biochemically validated where possible, unless stated otherwise.

## 1. Combination NRT vs single NRT vs varenicline (Cochrane NMA effect sizes and absolute 6-month quit rates)

### Takeaway
The best ranked options, all with high-certainty evidence, are varenicline, cytisine and nicotine e-cigarettes (OR about 2.2–2.4 vs no medicine). Combination NRT (patch plus a fast-acting form) is close behind (OR 1.93) and its CrI overlaps with theirs. Head-to-head trials show no clear quit-rate difference between varenicline and combination NRT (RR 1.02). A single form of NRT is clearly weaker (OR about 1.4). In absolute terms, about 6 in 100 people quit for 6+ months with no medicine, about 9 in 100 with single NRT, about 12 in 100 with combination NRT and about 14 in 100 with varenicline. The user's chosen regimen is one of the top-tier options.

### Cited Findings
- **Lindson et al., Cochrane cNMA, 2023 (Issue 9).** 319 RCTs, 157,179 participants, searches to April 2022. Odds ratios vs control (no medicine or placebo):
  - Nicotine e-cigarettes: OR 2.37 (95% CrI 1.73–3.24; 16 RCTs, 3828 participants; high certainty).
  - Varenicline: OR 2.33 (2.02–2.68; 67 RCTs, 16,430; high).
  - Cytisine: OR 2.21 (1.66–2.97; 7 RCTs, 3848; high).
  - Combination NRT (patch plus fast-acting form): OR 1.93 (1.61–2.34). This is a calculated additive effect with certainty not rated. Its point estimate is lower than the top three but the CrIs overlap.
  - Nicotine patch alone: OR 1.37 (1.20–1.56; 105 RCTs, 37,319; high).
  - Fast-acting NRT alone: OR 1.41 (1.29–1.55; 120 RCTs, 31,756; high).
  - Bupropion: OR 1.43 (1.26–1.62; 71 RCTs, 14,759; high).
  - Nortriptyline: OR 1.35 (1.02–1.81; moderate).
  - Non-nicotine e-cigarettes: OR 1.16 (0.74–1.80; low).
  — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/); [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD015226.pub2/full)
- **Same review, absolute effects (extra quitters per 100 vs control):**
  - E-cigarettes +8 (95% CrI 4–13); varenicline +8 (6–10); cytisine +7 (4–12).
  - Patch +2 (1–3); fast-acting NRT +3 (2–3); bupropion +3 (2–4).
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)
- **Summary-of-findings table, varenicline:** 6 per 100 quit with control vs 14 per 100 (12–16) with varenicline. That is 8 more per 100 (6–10), with a prediction interval of 1.31–4.11. — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD015226.pub2/full)
- **Cochrane press release on that review:** about 12 in 100 people using two forms of NRT together quit successfully, vs about 9 in 100 using one form. The combination NRT estimate is less certain because it was calculated from the effects of the individual NRT components rather than taken directly from trials of the combination. — [Cochrane news](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/e-cigarettes-varenicline-and-cytisine-are-most-effective-stop-smoking-aids-analysis-over-150000)
- **Same review, other findings:**
  - Patch and fast-acting NRT did not differ from each other.
  - Varenicline, bupropion, nortriptyline, fast-acting NRT and combination NRT caused more treatment withdrawals than placebo.
  - SAEs (serious adverse events) averaged about 3% overall. Low-certainty evidence showed no clear SAE difference vs control for e-cigarettes, varenicline, cytisine or NRT. Bupropion may cause about 1 more SAE per 100 (0–2).
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/); [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD015226.pub2/full)
- **Direct pairwise comparisons (Livingstone-Banks et al., Cochrane, 5 May 2023; 75 trials, 45,049 people):**
  - Varenicline vs single-form NRT: RR 1.25 (1.14–1.37; 11 studies, 7572; high certainty).
  - Varenicline vs dual-form (combination) NRT: RR 1.02 (0.87–1.20; 5 studies, 2344; low certainty, imprecise). This is no clear difference.
  - Varenicline vs placebo: RR 2.32 (2.15–2.51; 41 studies, 17,395; high).
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37142273/)
- **Combination vs single-form NRT (Lindson et al., Cochrane "doses, durations and modes of delivery of NRT", 18 Apr 2019):**
  - RR 1.25 (1.15–1.36; 14 studies, 11,356; high certainty).
  - 4 mg gum beat 2 mg gum: RR 1.43 (1.12–1.83), with the benefit seemingly limited to highly dependent smokers.
  - 42/44 mg patches were no better than 21/22 mg: RR 1.09 (0.93–1.29).
  - Fast-acting NRT and patch gave similar results: RR 0.90 (0.77–1.05).
  — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308/full)
- **Any NRT vs control (Hartmann-Boyce et al., Cochrane, 31 May 2018; 133 trials, 64,640 participants):** RR 1.55 (1.49–1.61), high quality. By form:
  - Gum 1.49 (1.40–1.60).
  - Patch 1.64 (1.53–1.75).
  - Lozenge or tablet 1.52 (1.32–1.74).
  - Inhalator 1.90 (1.36–2.67).
  - Nasal spray 2.02 (1.49–2.73).
  The effect did not depend on how much extra support was given. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29852054/)
- **Real-world comparison (Baker et al., JAMA, 2016; open-label RCT, n=1086, 12 weeks of treatment):** no significant differences between treatments. 7-day point-prevalence abstinence:
  - At 26 weeks: patch 22.8%, varenicline 23.6%, combination NRT 26.8%.
  - At 52 weeks: patch 20.8%, varenicline 19.1%, combination NRT 20.2%.
  Varenicline caused more vivid dreams, insomnia, nausea, constipation, sleepiness and indigestion than the patch. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26813210/)
- **NICE NG209 (last updated 4 Feb 2025)** advises that, combined with behavioural support, these options are "more likely to result in them successfully stopping smoking": cytisinicline, "a combination of short-acting and long-acting NRT", varenicline and nicotine-containing e-cigarettes. Bupropion and either NRT form used alone are "less likely". — [NICE NG209, Treating tobacco dependence](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **WHO 2024 guideline:**
  - Strong recommendations for varenicline, NRT, bupropion and cytisine.
  - Varenicline, NRT or bupropion are first-line. Combination NRT (patch plus a short-acting form) is "an option".
  - Certainty: high for varenicline, NRT and bupropion; moderate for combination NRT and cytisine.
  — [WHO guideline PDF](https://washingtonbreathes.org/resources/view/66/WHO_tobacco_cessation_guidelines_2024.pdf); [WHO news, 2 Jul 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-07-2024-who-releases-first-ever-clinical-treatment-guideline-for-tobacco-cessation-in-adults)

### Inferences
- For a motivated relapser, combination NRT is a well-evidenced first-line choice. Its likely quit rate sits within the CrIs of varenicline and cytisine, and direct trials (RR 1.02; Baker 2016) show no clear difference from varenicline. Absolute 6-month quit rates in trials are modest whatever the method: roughly 10–25% depending on setting and support. Repeated attempts are normal.
- Using a full dose matters more than which product. Supporting points: 4 mg gum beats 2 mg for heavier smokers, 21 mg patches beat 14 mg, and NICE stresses getting a "high enough dose".

### Gaps
- I found no Cochrane cNMA update after the 2023 version; searches ran to April 2022. The combination NRT estimate is a modelled additive effect, not a pooled direct estimate.
- Absolute rates depend on the control-group rate (6/100 in Cochrane), so real-world clinic rates differ.

## 2. Pre-quit NRT ("preloading"): does starting a patch 1–4 weeks before quit day help?

### Takeaway
Moderate-certainty evidence says starting NRT before quit day modestly improves long-term abstinence: RR 1.25 in Cochrane 2019. The largest trial (UK Preloading, BMJ 2018) found a similar but non-significant effect unadjusted (OR 1.25 at 6 months). The effect became significant after adjustment, because preloaders used less varenicline afterwards. Preloading causes more nausea and other nicotine-excess symptoms. NICE now says to provide NRT before the quit date, ready to start the day before.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane (Lindson 2019):** NRT before quit day vs from quit day: RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.08–1.44; 9 studies, 4395 participants; I² = 0%). Certainty was moderate, limited by risk of bias. The authors note "further research is needed to ensure the robustness of this finding". — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308/full)
- **UK Preloading trial (Preloading Investigators, BMJ, 13 Jun 2018).** Design: pragmatic open-label RCT; 1792 daily smokers; primary care and stop-smoking clinics in England, 2012–15; 21 mg/24h patch for about 4 weeks before quit day.
  - 6-month Russell Standard abstinence: 17.5% (157/899) preloading vs 14.4% (129/893) control; OR 1.25 (0.97–1.62), P=0.08.
  - 12-month abstinence: 14.0% vs 11.3%; OR 1.28 (0.97–1.69).
  - Varenicline use after quitting was lower in the preloading arm (22.1% vs 29.5%). Adjusted ORs: 1.27 (1.03–1.57) at 6 months and 1.30 (1.02–1.66) at 12 months, a risk difference of about 3–4 percentage points.
  - Harms: 5.9% stopped preloading due to intolerance. Nicotine-excess symptoms were 45.5% vs 32.7% (nausea, dizziness, palpitations). Excess nausea was 2.5 percentage points. SAEs were equal (8 vs 8).
  — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5998048/); [BMJ abstract](https://www.bmj.com/content/361/bmj.k2164.abstract)
- **Cochrane cNMA (2023)** modelled timing relative to quit day as a component. Its abstract does not give a separate preloading estimate. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)
- **NICE NG209 1.12.10:** "For adults, prescribe or provide bupropion, cytisinicline, NRT or varenicline before they stop smoking... NRT, agree a quit date and ensure the person has NRT ready to start the day before the quit date." — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **Varenicline analogue:** a 4-week pre-quit varenicline run-in was no better than the standard 1-week run-in (JAMA Netw Open, Nov 2022; n=320). End-of-treatment continuous abstinence was 39.3% vs 36.3% (OR 1.13; 0.72–1.78), and 6-month results were similar. Pre-quit smoking fell more with the longer run-in (−38.8% vs −17.5%). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36367720/)

### Inferences
- With quit day on 26 Oct and the research date 22 Oct, only about 4 days of preloading are possible. The trials used about 2–4 weeks, so the benefit of a few days is unknown. Starting the patch now, while still smoking, is probably low-risk. Expect possible nausea or "too much nicotine" symptoms, and reduce cigarettes if they occur. The main practical message is to have the products in hand before Monday.

### Gaps
- I found no 2023–2026 RCT or Cochrane update on NRT preloading specifically.
- There is no evidence on preloading durations under 1–2 weeks.

## 3. Cytisine and cytisinicline: efficacy, US FDA status, and availability by country

### Takeaway
Cytisine (called cytisinicline in the UK/US) is effective, cheap and better tolerated than varenicline. In the Cochrane cNMA it is high-certainty and about as effective as varenicline (OR 2.21). The pairwise Cochrane review gives a smaller, heterogeneous estimate (RR 1.30). Head-to-head data slightly favour varenicline (RR 0.83, CI crosses 1).
- **US:** not approved as of Oct 2026. The FDA issued a Complete Response Letter (CRL) on its 20 June 2026 PDUFA date (announced 22 June 2026) for third-party manufacturing deficiencies only. Achieve plans to resubmit in Q4 2026, with potential approval in the first half of 2027.
- **Elsewhere:** prescription-only in the UK since early 2024 (NICE-recommended since Feb 2025); over the counter in Canada (natural health product) and Poland; available in much of Europe and New Zealand. Added to the WHO Essential Medicines List on 5 Sep 2025.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane cNMA 2023:** cytisine vs control OR 2.21 (95% CrI 1.66–2.97; 7 RCTs, 3848; high certainty); about 7 extra quitters per 100 (4–12). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)
- **Cochrane NRPA review (Livingstone-Banks, May 2023):**
  - Cytisine vs placebo: RR 1.30 (1.15–1.47; I² = 83%; 4 studies, 4623; moderate certainty).
  - Cytisine vs varenicline: RR 0.83 (0.66–1.05; 2 studies, 2131; moderate). This favours varenicline but is imprecise.
  - SAEs, cytisine vs varenicline: RR 0.67 (0.44–1.03; low). Authors: "Cytisine may lead to fewer people reporting SAEs than varenicline."
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37142273/)
  - Conflict: the cNMA OR (2.21) and the pairwise RR (1.30) differ markedly because of heterogeneity and different trial sets. A 2026 commentary says meta-analyses show cytisine increases cessation "approximately 2.6 fold" vs placebo. NCSCT (March 2026) says "approximately 75%". — [Polosa & Cohen, iScience, 4 Feb 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12927057/); [NCSCT cytisinicline briefing](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Cytisine.pdf)
- **ORCA-3 (Rigotti et al., JAMA, 11 Jul 2023):** US phase 3 trial; n=810; cytisinicline 3 mg three times daily vs placebo, plus behavioural support.
  - 6-week course: continuous abstinence 25.3% vs 4.4% (weeks 3–6; OR 8.0, 95% CI 3.9–16.3) and 8.9% vs 2.6% (weeks 3–24; OR 3.7, 1.5–10.2).
  - 12-week course: 32.6% vs 7.0% (weeks 9–12; OR 6.3, 3.7–11.6) and 21.1% vs 4.8% (weeks 9–24; OR 5.3, 2.8–11.1).
  - Nausea, abnormal dreams and insomnia each occurred in fewer than 10%. 2.9% discontinued for adverse events. No drug-related SAEs.
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432430/)
- **Tolerability vs varenicline:** nausea 10.3% with cytisine vs 25.1% with varenicline, as cited in a 2026 review. — [Polosa & Cohen 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12927057/)
- **Cytisine vs NRT and vs varenicline trials, as summarised by NCSCT (Mar 2026):**
  - A New Zealand trial found cytisine more effective than NRT.
  - A New Zealand trial found 12 weeks of cytisine similar to varenicline.
  - An Australian trial found 25 days of cytisine "probably less effective than 12 weeks of varenicline".
  - Side effects are less common than with varenicline.
  — [NCSCT](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Cytisine.pdf)
- **US FDA timeline (cytisinicline, Achieve Life Sciences):**
  - The NDA was accepted in September 2025 with a PDUFA target date of 20 June 2026. — [Achieve press release](https://achievelifesciences.com/achieve-announces-fda-acceptance-of-cytisinicline-nda/)
  - 22 June 2026: Achieve received a CRL. It cites cGMP (manufacturing standards) observations at a prior third-party manufacturing facility, classified "Official Action Indicated", plus labelling not finalised by the action date. "The FDA identified no deficiencies regarding the clinical efficacy or safety."
  - Achieve has moved manufacturing to Adare Pharma Solutions (US). It intends to resubmit the NDA in Q4 2026, "with potential FDA approval in the first half of 2027".
  - The safety database covers over 1,500 exposed participants, including over 400 with 6+ months' and over 200 with 1+ year's cumulative exposure.
  - The FDA has also granted Breakthrough Therapy designation and a Commissioner's National Priority Voucher for a vaping-cessation indication.
  — [Achieve CRL release](https://achievelifesciences.com/achieve-receives-complete-response-letter-from-fda-for-cytisinicline-nda/); [Reuters, 22 Jun 2026](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/us-fda-declines-approve-achieve-life-sciences-smoking-cessation-drug-2026-06-22/); [AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/fda-issues-complete-response-letter-for-cytisinicline-nda-citing-manufacturing-deficiencies)
- **UK:** prescription-only medicine, available since early 2024 (Consilient Health's product was first; Belnifrem is also MHRA-approved).
  - Regimen: 25 days with a quit date no later than day 5. Days 1–3: 1 tablet every 2 hours (max 6/day). Tapering down to 1–2 tablets/day on days 21–25. Each tablet is 1.5 mg; one pack of 100 tablets is one course.
  — [NCSCT, Mar 2026](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Cytisine.pdf); [Pharmaceutical Journal](https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/smoking-cessation-medicine-cytisine-to-be-available-in-january-2024-distributor-confirms)
  - Conflict on the MHRA licence date: the WHO EML application says the MHRA licensed cytisine in August 2023; Polosa & Cohen say 2019. — [WHO EML application](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/2025-eml-expert-committee/addition-of-new-medicines/a.8_cytisine.pdf); [Polosa & Cohen 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12927057/)
- **NICE NG209 (Feb 2025 update):**
  - Adds cytisinicline to the products that must be accessible (1.12.2) and to the "more likely" to work list (1.12.8).
  - "Do not offer cytisinicline to people aged 66 and over" (1.12.5). Not for under-18s (1.12.4).
  - Agree a quit date within the first 5 days of treatment (1.12.10).
  — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **Canada:** approved by Health Canada in August 2017 as a natural health product, sold without prescription. — [CMAJ 2018](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/190/19/e596)
  - The Canadian Task Force (25 Aug 2025) strongly recommends cytisine. — [CMAJ 2025](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/197/28/E846)
- **OTC and other countries:** over the counter in Canada and Poland; long available without prescription in Central and Eastern Europe, and in "much of Europe, New Zealand and Canada". — [NCSCT](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Cytisine.pdf)
  - Italy has galenic preparations (about €30–35 per month) and a branded generic (Defucitan, approved 2023). Cytisine "has remained unavailable in the US". — [Polosa & Cohen 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12927057/)
- **WHO:** cytisine was added to the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines (24th list) on 5 Sep 2025. — [Polosa & Cohen 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12927057/)
  - The WHO 2024 guideline gives cytisine a strong recommendation with moderate certainty. It was listed separately because of variable dosing and limited regulatory approval. — [WHO guideline PDF](https://washingtonbreathes.org/resources/view/66/WHO_tobacco_cessation_guidelines_2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the user: if combination NRT fails or he prefers a non-nicotine tablet, cytisine is a good, cheap alternative where available (UK, Canada, much of the EU, NZ). It is not an option in the US until at least 2027.

### Gaps
- I could not verify Australian availability or regulatory status for cytisine in this research. I believe it is not TGA-registered, but this is unconfirmed.
- I did not retrieve exact numbers for the NZ cytisine vs NRT and cytisine vs varenicline trials, or the Australian cytisine vs varenicline trial.

## 4. Varenicline: availability after the 2021 nitrosamine recall, safety, dosing, and quit-date approach

### Takeaway
- **Availability:** varenicline is available again in most markets.
  - US: generic varenicline since 2021; brand Chantix has been discontinued since the recall.
  - UK: generic since August 2024; Pfizer's Champix back since July 2025.
  - Canada and Australia: generics were available.
- **Effectiveness:** the most effective single prescription drug in pairwise Cochrane data. It beats single NRT and bupropion, and shows no clear difference from combination NRT.
- **Safety:** EAGLES (8144 participants) found no significant excess of neuropsychiatric adverse events vs patch or placebo. Nausea (about 25%) is the main side effect.
- **Dosing:** standard 12 weeks, with an optional further 12. Quit date is usually day 8–14 (start 1–2 weeks before quitting). A flexible quit date (quit between weeks 2 and 5) works similarly.

### Cited Findings
- **US recall and supply:**
  - The FDA recall notice covers Pfizer's expansion of the voluntary US recall to all lots of Chantix due to N-nitroso-varenicline content. — [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts/pfizer-expands-voluntary-nationwide-recall-include-all-lots-chantixr-varenicline-tablets-due-n)
  - "In September 2021, all lots of Chantix were recalled by Pfizer. Shortly thereafter, the first FDA-approved generic varenicline became available." — [PMC study of US prescribing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12543408/)
  - Brand Chantix "has been fully discontinued since its recall", but FDA-approved generics are available. — [GoodRx](https://www.goodrx.com/chantix/chantix-production-halted)
- **England:** generic varenicline was "available in the United States, Canada and Australia, but a generic was not licensed and supplied in England up to February 2024". — [Addiction/PMC population study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11753482/)
  - NCSCT: Champix was recalled in October 2021; "Generic varenicline was introduced in August 2024, and Champix was reintroduced in July 2025". The reintroduced Champix meets current nitrosamine limits. — [NCSCT varenicline briefing (dated June 2026)](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Varenicline-2025.pdf)
- **Dosing (NCSCT):**
  - Days 1–3: 0.5 mg once daily. Days 4–7: 0.5 mg twice daily. Day 8 onward: 1 mg twice daily, after food.
  - The dose can be lowered to 0.5 mg twice daily if not tolerated.
  - Quit date is usually day 8–14.
  - Standard course is 12 weeks; an extra 12 weeks (24 total) may be considered for successful quitters to maintain abstinence. A 2-week taper at 0.5 mg twice daily may be considered.
  - A gradual approach, reducing over 12 weeks and quitting by the end, can be considered for people unable or unwilling to quit abruptly.
  — [NCSCT](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Varenicline-2025.pdf)
  - NICE 1.12.10: "agree a quit date and start the treatment 1 to 2 weeks before this date". — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **Flexible quit date (Rennard et al., Nicotine Tob Res, 2012; n=659):** varenicline vs placebo with quit day chosen between days 8 and 35. Continuous abstinence was 53.1% vs 19.3% at weeks 9–12 (OR 5.9) and 34.7% vs 12.7% at weeks 9–24 (OR 4.4). "Similar efficacy and safety compared with previous fixed quit date studies." — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22080588/)
- **Extended pre-quit run-in:** extending pre-quit varenicline from 1 to 4 weeks did not improve abstinence (OR 1.13; 0.72–1.78). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36367720/)
- **EAGLES (Anthenelli et al., Lancet, 2016; n=8144):**
  - Non-psychiatric cohort, moderate or severe neuropsychiatric adverse events: varenicline 1.3%, bupropion 2.2%, patch 2.5%, placebo 2.4%.
  - Psychiatric cohort: 6.5%, 6.7%, 5.2% and 4.9% respectively. Risk differences were not significant.
  - Abstinence ORs for varenicline: 3.61 vs placebo (3.07–4.24), 1.68 vs patch (1.46–1.93), 1.75 vs bupropion (1.52–2.01).
  - Nausea occurred in 25% of varenicline users.
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27116918/)
- **Cochrane 2023 harms:**
  - Varenicline vs placebo SAEs: RR 1.23 (1.01–1.48; moderate certainty).
  - Cardiac SAEs: RR 1.20 (0.79–1.84; low). Neuropsychiatric SAEs: RR 0.89 (0.61–1.29; low). Both are imprecise.
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37142273/)

### Inferences
- If the user wanted a non-nicotine option (for example, because nicotine dependence itself worries him), varenicline is available and effective. It also offers a flexible-quit-date route. However, it is not clearly superior to his combination NRT plan.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve 2024–2026 RCTs on combining varenicline with NRT, or on varenicline dose extension.
- I did not verify current Canadian and Australian brand vs generic availability beyond the 2025 study's statement.

## 5. Bupropion: where does it rank now?

### Takeaway
Bupropion is effective vs placebo (OR 1.43, high certainty). It is about as effective as a single form of NRT, clearly less effective than varenicline, and below combination NRT and cytisine. NICE lists it among the "less likely to succeed" options. WHO still recommends it as first-line, plus conditionally in combination when first-line treatment fails. It may slightly increase SAEs.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane cNMA 2023:**
  - Bupropion OR 1.43 (1.26–1.62; 71 RCTs, 14,759; high certainty), about +3 quitters per 100.
  - Bupropion "may slightly increase rates of SAEs" (moderate certainty), about +1 per 100 (0–2).
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)
- **Head-to-head:**
  - Varenicline vs bupropion: RR 1.36 (1.25–1.49; 9 studies, 7560; high certainty). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37142273/)
  - EAGLES: varenicline vs bupropion OR 1.75 (1.52–2.01). Bupropion vs placebo OR 2.07 (1.75–2.45). Insomnia occurred in 12% on bupropion. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27116918/)
- **NICE 1.12.9:** bupropion is among the options "less likely to result in them successfully stopping smoking". — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **WHO 2024:**
  - Bupropion is first-line (strong recommendation, high certainty).
  - Bupropion plus NRT or plus varenicline gets a conditional recommendation (moderate certainty with varenicline, low with NRT) "when there is inadequate response to first-line treatments".
  — [WHO guideline PDF](https://washingtonbreathes.org/resources/view/66/WHO_tobacco_cessation_guidelines_2024.pdf)
- **Canadian Task Force (Aug 2025):** strong recommendation for bupropion along with NRT, varenicline and cytisine. — [CMAJ](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/197/28/E846)
  - Caution: an automated extraction of this page gave "128 more per 1000 (4 RCTs, n=404)" for bupropion. That looks inconsistent with Cochrane and should be checked before quoting.

### Inferences
- Bupropion is a second-tier option for this user. It is mainly relevant if he cannot use NRT, varenicline or cytisine, or as an add-on after failure.

### Gaps
- None material.

## 6. E-cigarettes: latest Cochrane living review, dual-use and long-term nicotine concerns, and guidance (UK vs US/WHO)

### Takeaway
- **Evidence:** the latest Cochrane living review (Lindson et al., Nov 2025, evidence to 1 Mar 2025) finds high-certainty evidence that nicotine e-cigarettes beat NRT: RR 1.55 (1.28–1.88). That is about 9 vs 6 quitters per 100, so +3 per 100. There is no detected excess of adverse events vs NRT and no evidence of serious harm, but long-term (more than 2 years) data are lacking.
- **Continued nicotine use:** this is the key trade-off. In the landmark NEJM trial, 80% of e-cigarette-arm quitters were still vaping at 1 year, vs 9% of NRT-arm quitters still using NRT.
- **UK:** endorses vaping (NICE; Swap to Stop free kits).
- **WHO:** e-cigarettes are outside the scope of its 2024 cessation guideline.
- **Canada:** the Task Force (2025) conditionally recommends against vaping for most people.
- **US:** USPSTF (2021) found evidence "insufficient"; no e-cigarette is FDA-approved as a cessation aid.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane living review (Lindson et al., CD010216.pub10, published online 10 Nov 2025, Issue 11; searches to 1 Mar 2025; 104 studies, 61 RCTs, 30,366 participants):**
  - Nicotine e-cigarettes vs NRT: RR 1.55 (95% CI 1.28–1.88; I² = 0%; 9 studies, 2703; high certainty). That is 9 per 100 (8–11) vs 6 per 100, an extra 3 per 100 (2–5).
  - Nicotine vs non-nicotine e-cigarettes: RR 1.34 (1.06–1.70; 7 studies, 1918; moderate). That is 8 vs 6 per 100.
  - Nicotine e-cigarettes vs behavioural support or no support: RR 1.78 (1.42–2.25; 11 studies, 6819; lower certainty). That is 7 vs 4 per 100.
  - Adverse events vs NRT: RR 1.00 (0.73–1.37). Serious adverse events vs NRT: RR 1.22 (0.73–2.03), wide CIs.
  - Authors: "We did not detect evidence of serious harm from nicotine EC, but longer, larger trials are needed." Illicit or THC products may differ.
  - The review will continue as a living review "until at least 2027".
  — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub10/full); [Cochrane plain-language summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD010216_can-electronic-cigarettes-help-people-stop-smoking-and-do-they-have-any-unwanted-effects-when-used)
- **Previous version (Jan 2025, pub9):** conclusions unchanged. It included the first RCT comparing flavours with 6-month follow-up, which was inconclusive. — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub9/full)
- **Hajek et al., NEJM, 14 Feb 2019 (n=886, UK stop-smoking services):**
  - 1-year abstinence: 18.0% e-cigarette vs 9.9% NRT; RR 1.83 (1.30–2.58).
  - Among 1-year abstainers, 80% (63/79) in the e-cigarette arm were still using their e-cigarette at 52 weeks, vs 9% (4/44) in the NRT arm still using NRT.
  - The e-cigarette arm had more throat or mouth irritation (65.3% vs 51.2%). The NRT arm had more nausea (37.9% vs 31.3%).
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30699054/)
- **NICE NG209 (2021 recommendations, still current Feb 2025):** e-cigarettes are listed among the options more likely to succeed with behavioural support (1.12.8). Advisers should explain (1.12.15–1.12.16) that:
  - e-cigarettes are not licensed medicines;
  - "there is not enough evidence to know whether there are long-term harms from e-cigarette use";
  - they are "likely to be substantially less harmful than smoking";
  - "any smoking is harmful, so people using e-cigarettes should stop smoking tobacco completely" (an implicit warning against dual use);
  - advisers should discuss "how long the person intends to use nicotine-containing e-cigarettes" and "how to stop using them when they are ready".
  - As of Feb 2025, no e-cigarette was MHRA-licensed as a medicine and commercially available.
  — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **England's "Swap to Stop":**
  - Free vape starter kits plus behavioural support, funded for almost 1 in 5 people who smoke. It ran initially to March 2025, with a one-year extension announced in March 2025.
  - In early data, 37% were confirmed quit at 4 weeks.
  - A population survey (Addiction, 2026) found the scheme associated with more quit attempts using vapes.
  — [PMC (Addiction 2026)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13155265/)
  - King's College London reported the scheme saw "approximately 125,000 more people using vapes to try and quit". — [KCL news](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/government-swap-to-stop-scheme-sees-approximately-125000-more-people-using-vapes-to-try-and-quit-smoking-cigarettes)
- **WHO 2024 guideline:** "E-cigarettes are beyond the scope of this guideline because the potential benefits and harms of using these products are complex... These products may be addressed in the future as evidence accumulates." — [WHO guideline PDF](https://washingtonbreathes.org/resources/view/66/WHO_tobacco_cessation_guidelines_2024.pdf)
- **USPSTF (quoted in the Cochrane review background):** "the current evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of electronic cigarettes (e‑cigarettes) for tobacco cessation in adults". — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD010216.pub10/full)
- **US approval status:** the CDC lists FDA-approved cessation medicines as NRT, varenicline and bupropion; e-cigarettes are not among them. — [CDC clinical interventions](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/hcp/patient-care-settings/clinical.html)
- **Canadian Task Force on Preventive Health Care (25 Aug 2025):** "conditional recommendation against using e-cigarettes for smoking cessation" for most people. It "may be considered for people who have unsuccessfully attempted other interventions or express a strong preference". — [CMAJ](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/197/28/E846)
- **Australia (newsGP, 15 Jul 2025):** a clinical trial "found vapes almost three times more effective than nicotine lozenges or gum". This is a headline snippet only; I did not retrieve the trial details. — [newsGP](https://www1.racgp.org.au/newsgp/clinical/vapes-gums-or-lozenges-for-smoking-cessation)

### Inferences
- Vaping modestly beats NRT for quitting cigarettes. The price is that most successful vapers are still using nicotine at a year, whereas almost all successful NRT users have stopped. For someone whose explicit goal is to be nicotine-free by Day 90, NRT's low rate of persisting use is an advantage, not a drawback.
- Dual use (smoking plus vaping) does not deliver the health benefit. All guidance stresses stopping tobacco completely.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve quantitative data on how common long-term dual use is among people who try vaping to quit, or on the health effects of dual use. (The Cochrane review notes that toxicant biomarkers are lower in exclusive vapers than in smokers or dual users, but I did not extract numbers.)
- I did not retrieve the text of the current US FDA/CDC position statements on vaping for cessation.

## 7. Nicotine pouches (Zyn etc.): quit aid or new dependence?

### Takeaway
There is essentially no evidence that nicotine pouches help people quit smoking. The 2025 Cochrane review found only 4 tiny studies (n=284), very low or low certainty, with no demonstrable cessation benefit. One small study hinted pouches may be less effective than e-cigarettes. They may reduce cigarettes per day and seem well tolerated short-term. No regulator has approved them as a cessation aid. The FDA authorised Zyn as a tobacco product in January 2025, later with a modified-risk claim. They are commonly used alongside other nicotine products, are seen as low-harm, and appeal to young adult males. They are better seen as an alternative nicotine product with dependence potential than as a quit aid.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane (Hartmann-Boyce et al., CD016220.pub2, online 24 Oct 2025): 4 small studies, n=284.**
  - Pouches vs minimal control: RR 1.58 (95% CI 0.07–35.32; very low certainty).
  - Pouches vs e-cigarettes: RR 0.25 (0.03–2.02; low certainty).
  - Pouches vs NRT: cessation not measured.
  - No SAEs were reported.
  - Conclusion: "limited evidence on the use of ONP for cessation or reduction of cigarette use... people randomised to ONP may be slightly less likely to quit smoking than those randomised to e-cigarettes."
  — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD016220.pub2/full)
- **Heshmati et al., Addiction (online 26 Sep 2025), systematic review of 7 studies (n=24–63 each):**
  - "None demonstrated a statistically significant increase in smoking cessation compared with control, snus or gum."
  - One pilot trial saw cigarettes per day fall from 15.0 to 8.3 over 8 weeks.
  - Adverse events were mild and more frequent at higher doses.
  — [Addiction](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/add.70193)
- **Fucito et al., Tobacco Control (23 Jun 2025), pilot RCT, n=30, 6 mg vs 3 mg pouches:** complete abstinence 13% vs 0% (not significant). Willingness to keep using pouches 67% vs 40%. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40550628/)
- **Ottawa Heart Institute (26 Sep 2025):** "New study shows nicotine pouches may reduce smoking but do not help people quit". Headline only; I did not retrieve details. — [Ottawa Heart](https://www.ottawaheart.ca/news/new-study-shows-nicotine-pouches-may-reduce-smoking-do-not-help-people-quit)
- **Prevalence and dependence context (Addiction, online 12 Jun 2026; 16 studies, n=184,497):**
  - US current use ranged from 0.8% to 12.0%; Europe 0.06–4.3%.
  - Use was highest in adults aged 18–40 and in males.
  - Pouches are "frequently used concurrently with cigarettes, e-cigarettes, and smokeless tobacco" and are perceived as having "lower harm, lower addictiveness".
  - Marketing about flavours or cessation increased appeal.
  — [Addiction, doi 10.1111/add.70506](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.70506)
- **US regulation:**
  - 16 Jan 2025: the FDA authorised marketing of 20 ZYN pouch products (3 mg and 6 mg) via the tobacco premarket (PMTA) pathway. This was the first-ever pouch authorisation. — [FDA press release](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-authorizes-marketing-20-zyn-nicotine-pouch-products-after-extensive-scientific-review)
  - The FDA later issued modified-risk orders allowing the claim "Using ZYN instead of cigarettes puts you at a lower risk of mouth cancer, heart disease, lung cancer, stroke, emphysema, and chronic bronchitis". To date, 26 pouch products are authorised. — [FDA CTP newsroom](https://www.fda.gov/tobacco-products/ctp-newsroom/fda-authorizes-20-zyn-nicotine-pouches-be-marketed-specific-modified-risk-claim)
  - CDC: "The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved nicotine pouches as a smoking cessation aid." — [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/tobacco/nicotine-pouches/index.html)

### Inferences
- For this user, pouches are not an evidence-based quit tool. Licensed NRT gum or lozenges deliver oral nicotine with proven efficacy and a medicinal licence. Switching to pouches risks swapping one recreational nicotine habit for another.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT with 6-month cessation outcomes comparing pouches with NRT.
- I found no data on dependence or persistence rates among pouch users who switched from smoking.
- I could not confirm the exact date of the FDA modified-risk order.

## 8. "Don't nicotine patches keep me addicted?" Abuse liability, delivery speed, long-term use, safety, treatment length, extended duration, and taper vs abrupt stop

### Takeaway
**Direct answer: very rarely, and patches are the least likely nicotine product to do so.**
- Dependence potential rises with how fast a product delivers nicotine. Patches are the slowest, so they have the lowest abuse liability.
- In trials and purchase data, only about 1–2% of patch users are still using at 4–6 months. Faster products (gum, inhaler, spray) run at about 7–13%.
- In the NEJM vaping trial, only 9% of NRT-arm quitters were still using NRT at 1 year, vs 80% of vapers.
- Long-term NRT is considered far safer than smoking. NICE says licensed products "can be used for as long as they help stop them going back" to smoking. NRT showed no cancer signal in the Lung Health Study, and 52-week patch use was safe in an RCT.
- On duration: extending the patch from 8 to 24 weeks improved 6-month abstinence in one RCT. Going beyond 24 weeks added nothing. Cochrane finds no overall effect of patch duration (low certainty).
- On stopping: tapering vs stopping NRT abruptly makes little or no difference (Cochrane 2019: no effect; cNMA 2023: OR 1.14, 1.00–1.29, low certainty).
- **The real risk is relapse to cigarettes, not becoming dependent on the patch.**

### Cited Findings
- **Abuse liability vs delivery speed (West et al., Psychopharmacology, 2000; RCT of patch, gum, spray and inhaler):** continued NRT use at week 15 "was related to rate of delivery of nicotine from the products – 2% for patch, 7% for gum and inhaler, 10% for spray (P<0.05)". At week 15, "no patch users rated themselves as dependent". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10823399/)
- **Hajek, McRobbie & Gillison, Preventive Medicine, Mar 2007 (London stop-smoking service):**
  - "The rates of long-term use ranged from 2% for patch to 13% for nasal spray."
  - Long-term use was more likely in more dependent smokers.
  - Long-term use "seems positively related to speed of nicotine delivery".
  - "For self-selected highly dependent smokers, long-term use of NRT may be a necessary precondition for maintaining long-term abstinence."
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17207524/)
- **Shiffman et al., Tobacco Control, 2003 (US over-the-counter purchase panel):**
  - Continuous monthly purchase for 6+ months: 2.3% of new gum users and 0.9% of new patch users.
  - Allowing 1-month gaps: gum 6.7% at 6+ months and 1.0% at 24+ months; patch 1.7% and 0.05%.
  - "Persistent use of nicotine gum and patch is very rare."
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12958394/)
- **Persistence after quitting:** in the NEJM 2019 trial, 9% (4/44) of NRT-arm abstainers were still using NRT at 52 weeks, vs 80% of e-cigarette-arm abstainers still vaping. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30699054/)
- **Slower nicotine release:** NICE 1.12.13 advises using short-acting NRT "before the usual time they would have had the cigarette, to allow for the slower nicotine release from these products". — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **Safety of long-term NRT:**
  - Lung Health Study (Murray et al., Nicotine Tob Res, 2009): NRT use was not a significant predictor of lung cancer (p=0.57; p=0.25 adjusted for smoking), whereas smoking was (p=0.02–0.03). NRT also did not predict GI or all cancers. Surveillance time was short. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19571249/)
  - Schnoll et al., JAMA Intern Med, 2015 (n=525; patch for 8, 24 or 52 weeks): "The findings support the safety of long-term use of nicotine patch treatment". Duration was not associated with adverse effects. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705872/)
  - Cochrane NRT review (2018): NRT causes minor irritation at the site of use. Chest pain or palpitations had OR 1.88 (1.37–2.57) but were "rare in both groups". Serious adverse events were "extremely rare". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29852054/)
- **NICE NG209 on long-term use:**
  - 1.12.11: "most smoking-related health problems are caused by other components in tobacco smoke, not by the nicotine"; risks of licensed products "are much lower than those of smoking".
  - 1.15.4: licensed products are "a safe, effective way" to cut down and "can be used as a complete or partial substitute for tobacco, either in the short or long term".
  - 1.15.5: they "can be used for as long as they help stop them going back to previous levels of smoking".
  - 1.17.2: offer "a further course of varenicline, NRT or bupropion to prevent a relapse".
  — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
  - A NICE evidence summary (via search snippet) states licensed products are believed to be safe for "at least 5 years" and "lifetime use of medicinally licensed nicotine-containing products is likely to be considerably less harmful than smoking". This is a fragmentary snippet. — [NCBI Bookshelf (NICE NG209)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK588845/); [Medscape NICE 2025 summary](https://reference.medscape.com/cc2/p10/tobacco-promoting-quitting-and-treating-dependence-2022a10018l9)
- **Extended duration, 24 vs 8 weeks (Schnoll et al., Ann Intern Med, 2 Feb 2010; n=568):** results at week 24 for the extended arm vs the standard arm:
  - Point-prevalence abstinence 31.6% vs 20.3% (OR 1.81, 1.23–2.66).
  - Prolonged abstinence 41.5% vs 26.9% (OR 1.97).
  - Continuous abstinence 19.2% vs 12.6% (OR 1.64, 1.04–2.60).
  - Extended therapy lowered lapse risk (HR 0.77), raised recovery from lapses (HR 1.47) and slowed relapse (HR 0.50).
  - At week 52, only prolonged abstinence remained higher (P=0.027).
  - No difference in side effects.
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20124230/)
- **Beyond 24 weeks (Schnoll 2015):**
  - At 24 weeks: 27.2% (extended or maintenance) vs 21.7% (standard 8 weeks); unadjusted P=0.17, adjusted OR 1.70 (1.03–2.81).
  - At 52 weeks: maintenance (52 weeks of patch) vs others 20.3% vs 23.8% (OR 1.17, 0.69–1.98). Extended (24 weeks) vs standard 26.0% vs 21.7% (OR 1.33, 0.72–2.45).
  - "Do not support efficacy beyond 24 weeks".
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25705872/)
- **Cochrane 2019 on duration and tapering:** "We found no evidence of an effect of duration of nicotine patch use (low-certainty evidence)." "No evidence of an effect of tapering of patch dose versus abrupt patch cessation." — [Cochrane Library](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308/full)
- **Cochrane cNMA 2023 on tapering:** "low-certainty evidence that tapering the dose of NRT prior to stopping treatment may improve effectiveness". OR 1.14 (1.00–1.29; 111 RCTs, 33,156), about +1 quitter per 100 (0–2). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37696529/)

### Inferences
- **Answer for the user.** Patches deliver nicotine slowly and steadily, without the rapid "hit" that drives cigarette addiction. Patches have the lowest rate of persisting use of any nicotine product (about 1–2%). Even people who do use NRT long-term are far safer than if they smoked.
- **His 10–12 week step-down plan is reasonable and consistent with evidence.** Tapering is fine but optional. If cravings or near-lapses persist near Day 90, extending NRT (for example, to about 24 weeks) is supported by an RCT and by NICE. It is far preferable to stopping NRT on schedule and relapsing to cigarettes.
- **The gum or lozenge carries slightly more persistence risk than the patch (about 7% vs 2%).** Practical advice: phase out the fast-acting product deliberately, and do not let it become a habitual "treat".

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a primary pharmacokinetic source for arterial nicotine delivery times (cigarette seconds vs patch hours). The relation between delivery speed and dependence above is supported by West 2000 and Hajek 2007, but specific timing figures are unsourced in these notes.
- I found no 2023–2026 systematic review on long-term NRT use or abuse liability. Most data are older (2000–2015).
- UK and US product labelling on maximum NRT duration was not retrieved.

## 9. Cold turkey vs gradual reduction

### Takeaway
For people who want to quit, stopping abruptly on a set quit day beats cutting down first. In the UK RCT (Lindson-Hawley, Ann Intern Med 2016), 6-month abstinence was 22.0% vs 15.5%, with a 25% relative reduction at 6 months, and the advantage held even for people who preferred gradual reduction. Older pooled Cochrane data showed little difference (RR 0.94). A 2023/24 meta-analysis of 3 RCTs favoured abrupt cessation (RR 0.77). NICE says stopping "in one go" is the best approach. The user's plan of stopping cigarettes completely on 26 Oct with full-dose NRT matches this. Note that the evidence covers cigarettes, not tapering the NRT itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Lindson-Hawley et al., Ann Intern Med, 3 May 2016 (published online 15 Mar 2016).** Design: non-inferiority RCT; n=697; English primary care. The gradual group cut cigarettes by 75% over the 2 weeks before quit day. Both arms had nurse behavioural support and NRT before and after quit day.
  - 4-week abstinence: 39.2% gradual vs 49.0% abrupt; RR 0.80 (0.66–0.93).
  - 6-month abstinence: 15.5% vs 22.0%; RR 0.71 (0.46–0.91).
  - Among those who preferred gradual reduction, abrupt quitting still did better: 38.3% vs 52.2% abstinent at 4 weeks (P=0.007).
  - Conclusion: "Quitting smoking abruptly is more likely to lead to lasting abstinence than cutting down first, even for smokers who initially prefer to quit by gradual reduction."
  — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26975007/)
- **Earlier Cochrane review (2012, 10 RCTs, n=3760):** RR 0.94 (0.79–1.13), no significant difference. As summarised by Wiki Journal Club. — [Wiki Journal Club](https://www.wikijournalclub.org/wiki/Gradual_Vs._Abrupt_Smoking_Cessation)
- **Meta-analysis in Tobacco Induced Diseases (3 RCTs, n=1607):** prolonged abstinence was lower with gradual cessation (RR 0.77, 0.61–0.98), as was 7-day abstinence (RR 0.76, 0.61–0.94). — [Tobacco Induced Diseases](https://www.tobaccoinduceddiseases.org/A-meta-analysis-of-the-effectiveness-of-gradual-versus-abrupt-nsmoking-cessation,100557,0,2.html)
- **NICE 1.11.2:** "advise them that stopping smoking in one go is the best approach". — [NICE NG209](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng209/chapter/Treating-tobacco-dependence)
- **Varenicline exception:** NCSCT notes a gradual route (reduce over 12 weeks, quit by the end) can be considered for varenicline users unable or unwilling to quit abruptly. — [NCSCT](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Varenicline-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- For the user, stopping cigarettes completely on 26 Oct while on full-dose combination NRT is the best-supported approach. The gradual element of his plan applies to the NRT, which is fine.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the numbers in the 2019 Cochrane update of "Reduction versus abrupt cessation" (CD008033). I recall that it found no difference, but I could not confirm this and it should be checked before citing.

## 10. What is genuinely new in 2024–2026

### Takeaway
The main changes since 2023 are regulatory, availability and guidance changes, not new blockbuster efficacy data. The core efficacy rankings (varenicline ≈ cytisine ≈ e-cigarettes ≥ combination NRT > single NRT ≈ bupropion) are unchanged since the 2023 Cochrane reviews. Changes, in date order:
- WHO's first cessation guideline (Jul 2024).
- Cytisine launched in the UK (Jan 2024).
- UK varenicline returned: generic in Aug 2024, Champix in Jul 2025.
- FDA authorised ZYN pouches (Jan 2025).
- NICE added cytisinicline (Feb 2025).
- Canadian Task Force recommendations (Aug 2025).
- Cytisine added to the WHO Essential Medicines List (Sep 2025).
- First Cochrane review of nicotine pouches (Oct 2025).
- E-cigarette living-review updates (Jan and Nov 2025).
- US cytisinicline application received a CRL for manufacturing reasons (Jun 2026); resubmission planned Q4 2026.

### Cited Findings
- **Jan 2024:** cytisine became available in the UK as a prescription-only medicine. — [Pharmaceutical Journal](https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/smoking-cessation-medicine-cytisine-to-be-available-in-january-2024-distributor-confirms); [NCSCT](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Cytisine.pdf)

### Inferences
- Nothing published in 2024–2026 changes the recommendation that combination NRT, properly dosed and paired with behavioural support, is a top-tier choice. The newest practical change for someone in the UK, EU or Canada is easy access to cytisine as a cheap, well-tolerated alternative.

### Gaps
- I did not search for 2024–2026 trials of GLP-1 receptor agonists (for example semaglutide) or other novel agents for smoking cessation.
- I did not search for new 2024–2026 RCTs of combination NRT vs varenicline or e-cigarettes, or for whether the Cochrane NRT reviews (2018/2019) have been updated since. The latter remains an open gap.
- I did not retrieve USPSTF or CDC updates after 2021. The USPSTF 2021 recommendation is presumed still current.
