# Alcohol: guidance, abstinence vs moderation, medicines (incl. GLP-1), alcohol-free beer, withdrawal safety, weight (research as of 22 Oct 2026)

Context for the report writer: the notes are for a man who drinks too much beer, mostly socially at the golf course, and also smokes. He wants to lose weight. His plan is zero alcohol in weeks 1–2 (from Mon 26 Oct 2026). After that, beer stays off and he has one glass of wine with his wife on agreed evenings. His country is unknown, so the notes flag differences between countries. Standard-drink sizes differ: US 14 g of alcohol, Australia 10 g, UK unit 8 g (the UK figure is general knowledge, see Gaps).

## 1. What are the current drinking guidelines (as of Oct 2026)?

### Takeaway
Guidance has split. In January 2026 the US dropped numeric limits and now says only "consume less alcohol". Canada (2023) says 2 or fewer drinks a week is low risk. WHO says no level is safe. The UK keeps 14 units a week and Australia 10 standard drinks a week (4 a day at most). Every guideline agrees that less is better. The 2025 US Surgeon General advisory and the withdrawn ICCPUD draft both put measurable risk at about 1 drink a day or 7 a week. The NASEM review (Dec 2024) still found moderate drinking associated with lower all-cause mortality, at moderate certainty.

### Cited Findings
**US Dietary Guidelines 2025–2030 (released 7 Jan 2026)**
- Released 7 Jan 2026 at a White House press conference, after a six-month delay. The numeric caps (≤2 drinks/day for men, ≤1 for women) were dropped. The new text says "Limit alcoholic beverages" and "Consume less alcohol for better overall health". — [GV Wire](https://gvwire.com/2026/01/07/trump-administration-ditches-advice-to-limit-alcohol-to-1-2-drinks-per-day/); [AARP](https://www.aarp.org/health/healthy-living/dietary-guidelines-alcohol-older-adults/); [Beverage Daily, 8 Jan 2026](https://www.beveragedaily.com/Article/2026/01/08/us-drinking-guidelines-promote-moderation/)
- The 2025–2030 text tells three groups to avoid alcohol completely: pregnant women; people recovering from AUD or unable to control how much they drink; and people on medicines or with conditions that interact with alcohol. People with a family history of alcoholism are told to "be mindful". — [Food Business MEA](https://www.foodbusinessmea.com/us-drops-daily-alcohol-limits-in-2025-2030-dietary-guidelines-advises-americans-to-consume-less-alcohol/); [AASLD](https://www.aasld.org/aasld-raises-concern-over-removal-evidence-based-alcohol-guidance-2025-2030-dietary-guidelines)
- The new guidelines also drop the standard-drink definitions and the earlier references to cancer, liver disease and metabolic harm. — [HCPLive, 19 Jan 2026](https://www.hcplive.com/view/us-2025-2030-dietary-guidelines-addressing-alcohol-intake-recommendations)
- The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) expressed "deep concern" that specific guidance was removed. It also noted the guidelines are silent on alcohol and cancer. Industry groups welcomed the moderation framing. — [AASLD](https://www.aasld.org/aasld-raises-concern-over-removal-evidence-based-alcohol-guidance-2025-2030-dietary-guidelines); [Brewers Association](https://www.brewersassociation.org/government-affairs-updates/2025-2030-dietary-guidelines-for-americans-published/)

**US Surgeon General advisory on alcohol and cancer (3 Jan 2025, Vivek Murthy)**
- Alcohol contributes to about 100,000 US cancer cases and about 20,000 cancer deaths a year, more than the 13,500 alcohol-related traffic deaths. It is the third leading preventable cause of cancer after tobacco and obesity. It is causally linked to at least 7 cancers: breast, colorectum, oesophagus, liver, mouth, pharynx and larynx. — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/hematology-oncology/20250221/us-surgeon-general-calls-for-cancer-warnings-on-alcoholic-beverages); [HHS advisory PDF](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oash-alcohol-cancer-risk.pdf)
- For breast, mouth and throat cancers, risk "may start to increase around one or fewer drinks per day". The advisory calls for cancer warning labels and a "reassessment of the guideline limits". Fewer than half of US adults know about the alcohol–cancer link. — [Society of Behavioral Medicine](https://www.sbm.org/healthy-living/the-us-surgeon-generals-advisory-on-alcohol-and-cancer-risk-2025---what-does-it-mean); [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/hematology-oncology/20250221/us-surgeon-general-calls-for-cancer-warnings-on-alcoholic-beverages)

**NASEM "Review of Evidence on Alcohol and Health" (released 17 Dec 2024; NAP dates it 2025)**
- The report makes no recommendations. None of its conclusions reached high certainty. Its main findings:
  - **All-cause mortality:** moderate drinking is associated with lower mortality than never drinking (moderate certainty).
  - **Breast cancer:** moderate drinking is associated with higher risk of female breast cancer (moderate certainty).
  - **Colorectal cancer:** among moderate drinkers, higher amounts are associated with higher risk (low certainty).
  - **Cardiovascular mortality:** lower (moderate certainty). Lower non-fatal heart attack and stroke (low certainty).
  - **Weight:** no conclusion compared with never drinking.
  - **Dementia and oral or oesophageal cancer:** no conclusion.
  — [National Academies news release](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2024/12/new-report-reviews-evidence-on-moderate-alcohol-consumption-and-health-impacts)
- A secondary source puts the mortality difference at 16% lower all-cause mortality for moderate drinkers than for non-drinkers. — [Brewbound](https://www.brewbound.com/news/alcohol-consumption-study-spiked-from-dietary-guidelines-process) (not verified against the primary report)

**ICCPUD "Alcohol Intake and Health" draft (public comment 15 Jan–14 Feb 2025), later shelved**
- "Males and females have a 1 in 1,000 risk of dying from alcohol use if they consume more than seven drinks per week. This risk increases to 1 in 100 if they consume more than nine drinks per week." The draft found one drink a day raised the risk of cirrhosis, of oral and oesophageal cancer, and of injuries. Most of its evidence came from observational meta-analyses, with no Mendelian randomisation or RCTs. — [Report PDF (stopalcoholabuse.gov)](https://www.stopalcoholabuse.gov/media/pdf/Report-on-Alcohol-Intake-and-Health.pdf); [UPI, 15 Jan 2025](https://www.upi.com/Health_News/2025/01/15/alcohol-consumption-dangers-report/2781736968680/); [Federvini, 8 Sep 2025](https://www.federvini.it/en/news-en/u-s-congress-may-entirely-defund-iccpud-the-interagency-that-produced-a-report-during-the-biden-administration-for-the-dietary-guidelines-concluding-that-“the-risk-of-dying-from-alcohol-use-begins-at-low-levels-of-average-use)
- In early September 2025 HHS withdrew the report and did not send it to Congress. The NASEM report was used to inform the Dietary Guidelines instead. A House FY2026 draft bill proposed defunding ICCPUD. — [Brewbound](https://www.brewbound.com/news/alcohol-consumption-study-spiked-from-dietary-guidelines-process); [beSpacific](https://www.bespacific.com/?p=116987); [Federvini](https://www.federvini.it/en/news-en/u-s-congress-may-entirely-defund-iccpud-the-interagency-that-produced-a-report-during-the-biden-administration-for-the-dietary-guidelines-concluding-that-“the-risk-of-dying-from-alcohol-use-begins-at-low-levels-of-average-use)

**WHO (statement 4 Jan 2023, published in The Lancet Public Health)**
- "No level of alcohol consumption is safe for our health." Alcohol is an IARC Group 1 carcinogen that causes at least 7 cancers. About half of alcohol-attributable cancers in the WHO European Region come from "light and moderate" drinking. WHO defines that as under 1.5 L of wine, under 3.5 L of beer or under 450 ml of spirits a week. — [WHO news release](https://www.who.int/azerbaijan/news/item/04-01-2023-no-level-of-alcohol-consumption-is-safe-for-our-health)

**Canada: Canada's Guidance on Alcohol and Health (CCSA, Jan 2023)**
- Risk is treated as a continuum:
  - 0 drinks/week: no alcohol-related risk.
  - ≤2 standard drinks/week: low risk.
  - 3–6/week: moderate risk, with raised breast and colon cancer risk.
  - ≥7/week: increasingly high risk, including heart disease and stroke.
  - The 2011 guidance allowed 10/week for women and 15/week for men. — [CCSA poster PDF](https://www.ccsa.ca/sites/default/files/2024-05/Canadas-Guidance-on-Alcohol-and-Health-poster-2023-en.pdf); [Haldimand-Norfolk Health Unit](https://hnhu.org/health-topic/alcohol/canadas-guidance-on-alcohol-and-health-2023/)
- A 2025 Ontario study found 52% of drinkers exceeded the new low-risk threshold, compared with 11% under the old guidelines. — [Health Promotion and Chronic Disease Prevention in Canada, vol 45 no 2, 2025](https://www.canada.ca/en/public-health/services/reports-publications/health-promotion-chronic-disease-prevention-canada-research-policy-practice/vol-45-no-2-2025/increase-risk-classification-guidance-alcohol-health-examination-sample-community-adults-ontario.html)

**UK Chief Medical Officers (2016, still current)**
- To keep risk low, do not regularly drink more than 14 units a week (men and women alike). Spread it over 3 or more days, have several drink-free days, and do not "save up" units. One or two heavy sessions a week raise the risk of death from long-term illness and injury. No single-occasion limit is given. — [gov.scot factsheet](https://www.gov.scot/publications/lower-risk-drinking-guidelines-factsheet/); [CMO guidelines PDF (gov.wales)](https://www.gov.wales/sites/default/files/publications/2019-05/uk-chief-medical-officers-low-risk-drinking-guidelines.pdf); [Drinkaware](https://www.drinkaware.co.uk/low-risk-drinking-guidelines)

**Australia: NHMRC (published 8 Dec 2020, still current)**
- Healthy adults should drink no more than 10 standard drinks a week and no more than 4 on any one day. One standard drink is 10 g of alcohol. Within these limits the lifetime risk of dying from an alcohol-related condition is under 1 in 100. — [Health.gov.au](https://health.gov.au/news/australian-alcohol-guidelines-revised); [NSW Your Room](https://yourroom.health.nsw.gov.au/whats-new/Pages/2020-NHMRC-Guidelines.aspx); [NHMRC PDF](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/sites/default/files/2022-07/Australian-guidelines-to-reduce-health-risks-from-drinking-alcohol.pdf)

### Inferences
- **His wine plan.** One glass of wine on a few agreed evenings a week is likely 2–4 drinks a week. That is at or above Canada's "low risk" band (≤2/week) but well inside the UK (14 units) and Australian (10 drinks) limits. Under the WHO position, any amount carries some risk. The writer can say the plan is a large risk reduction compared with heavy beer drinking, but not "risk-free".
- **The US change is political, not new evidence.** The 2026 US guidance is looser in wording than the science bodies. The removal followed the shelving of the ICCPUD report, not new data showing safety. The direction of every guideline, including the US one, is "less".
- **The guidelines do not really conflict for this reader.** The NASEM mortality association rests on observational data and can be distorted by "sick quitters" and abstainer bias (general epidemiological caveat). For a heavy drinker who also smokes and wants to lose weight, there is no guideline in which cutting down is not beneficial.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the official 2025–2030 DGA document text (realfood.gov / dietaryguidelines.gov). The wording above comes from news and trade coverage.
- The exact NASEM percentage for mortality and its definition of "moderate" were not verified in the primary report. The 16% figure is secondary.
- There is no confirmed figure for the share of US adults aware of the alcohol–cancer link. "45%" was not verified; the sources say "less than half".
- No 2025–2026 revision of the UK or Australian guidelines was found. The UK unit (8 g) and the Canadian standard drink (13.45 g) are general knowledge and were not verified in this session.

## 2. Abstinence vs moderation, Dry January, and health markers after 1 month alcohol-free

### Takeaway
For people who drink too much but are not severely dependent, the evidence supports either goal. A meta-analysis found no significant difference between controlled-drinking and abstinence goals in RCTs. Drops in WHO risk drinking levels are tied to better long-term functioning. Temporary abstinence (Dry January) is not followed by rebound and is linked to lower drinking 6 months later. One month off alcohol produced large short-term improvements in insulin resistance, blood pressure, liver enzymes and weight (Mehta 2018). His plan of 2 weeks off then a lower-risk pattern fits this evidence.

### Cited Findings
**Abstinence vs controlled drinking**
- Henssler et al., Addiction, Aug 2021 (22 studies, including 5 RCTs, 4,204 patients):
  - RCTs: no significant difference between controlled-drinking and abstinence goals (OR 1.32, 95% CI 0.51–3.39).
  - Non-randomised free-choice studies favoured abstinence (OR 0.60, 0.40–0.90). This advantage disappeared with goal-specific treatment (OR 0.79, 0.40–1.56), in low-risk-of-bias studies (OR 0.73, 0.49–1.09) and with long follow-up (OR 1.49, 0.78–2.85).
  - "Effect sizes were not clearly dependent upon AUD severity." The authors conclude that controlled drinking "appears to be a viable option". — [PubMed 33188563](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33188563/)
- Witkiewitz et al., J Gen Intern Med 2021 (COMBINE and Project MATCH data): patients kept their drops in WHO risk drinking levels for up to 3 years after treatment. One- or two-level drops were associated with better mental health, quality of life and fewer consequences at 3 years. — [PubMed 33180306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33180306/)
- In COMBINE, 85.5% of people with at least a one-level drop at end of treatment kept it at 1 year. For two-level drops the figure was 77.8%. — [PMC (NIHMS1017868)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/mid/NIHMS1017868)
- Sustained drops of two or more WHO levels were associated with 44.0% lower healthcare costs over 3 years. A one-level drop was not significant. — [RTI](https://www.rti.org/publication/relationship-between-reductions-who-risk-drinking-levels-during-treatment-and-subsequent)
- A 2025 JAMA Psychiatry review by the ACTIVE workgroup (Witkiewitz, Anton, O'Malley et al., 82:1246–1254) argues for WHO risk-drinking-level reductions as a primary trial endpoint. It appears in the reference list of the 2026 Lancet semaglutide trial. — [Lancet SEMALCO reference list](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(26)00305-3/fulltext)

**Dry January / temporary abstinence**
- de Visser, Robinson & Bond, Health Psychology 2016;35(3):281–289 (857 UK adults; baseline, 1-month and 6-month follow-up):
  - At 6 months all respondents had lower alcohol use and higher drink-refusal self-efficacy, whether or not they completed the month. The changes were larger in those who completed it.
  - The best predictors of success were higher drink-refusal self-efficacy, more moderate baseline drinking, intending to stop, and fundraising.
  - The authors conclude periodic abstinence "is unlikely to cause rebound effects." — [EHPS abstract](https://ehps.net/ehp/index.php/contents/article/view/1141); [Sussex repository](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/57508/3/deVisser_etal_DryJanuary_inPress.pdf)
- de Visser's 2019 evaluation of Dry January (self-report). Participants reported:
  - 86% saved money
  - 81% felt more in control of their drinking
  - 70% slept better
  - 67% had better concentration
  - 66% had more energy
  - 54% lost weight
  - Using the free tools (Try Dry app, daily emails) roughly doubled the chance of completing an alcohol-free month. — [Alcohol Change UK evidence page](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/about-dry-january/the-evidence-behind-the-dry-january-challenge-benefits-and-long-term-impact)
- de Visser & Nicholls, Psychology & Health 2020: 70% of people who used the free tools had improved wellbeing and lower alcohol risk 6 months later. — [Alcohol Change UK](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/about-dry-january/the-evidence-behind-the-dry-january-challenge-benefits-and-long-term-impact)
- University of Sussex press release: 72% kept up lower levels of harmful drinking at 6 months, and 4% were still not drinking. This is press coverage. A Gizmodo report noted that one release was not peer reviewed and relied on self-report. — [Sussex news](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/news/article/50562-take-part-in-dry-january-and-you-ll-reap-the-benefits-for-months-sussex-research-shows); [Gizmodo AU](https://gizmodo.com.au/?p=1143818)
- An estimated 17.5 million people planned a month off alcohol in January 2026. Alcohol Change says NICE (2025) digital-tools guidance includes the Try Dry app. — [Alcohol Change UK](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/about-dry-january/the-evidence-behind-the-dry-january-challenge-benefits-and-long-term-impact)

**Health markers after 1 month alcohol-free (Mehta et al., BMJ Open 2018;8:e020673)**
- Design: prospective and non-randomised. 94 people chose to abstain for a month and 47 controls kept drinking. Baseline intake in the abstinence group was 258 g/week. Participants were healthy, and diet, exercise and smoking were self-reported (SLIQ).
- Changes in the abstinence group after 1 month (median):

| Measure | Change | Median before → after |
|---|---|---|
| Insulin resistance (HOMA) | −25.9% (p<0.001) | 1.4 → 1.0 |
| Systolic BP | −6.6% | 136 → 125 mmHg |
| Diastolic BP | −6.3% | 89 → 82 mmHg |
| Weight | −1.5% | 81.1 → 79.5 kg |
| GGT | −28.6% | — |
| ALT | −14.5% | — |
| Total cholesterol | −13.4% | — |
| VEGF | −41.8% | — |
| EGF | −73.9% | — |

- Controls showed no significant changes.
- Limitations: no randomisation, educated and health-motivated volunteers, possible lifestyle confounding, and no follow-up beyond 1 month. — [PMC5942469](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5942469)

### Inferences
- **The two-week reset is supported.** No rebound, a gain in self-efficacy and quick metabolic benefits make a short abstinence block a sensible reset. Two weeks is half the Mehta exposure, so expect smaller but same-direction changes. That is an extrapolation; no 2-week study was found.
- **The wine-only phase counts as harm reduction.** It is a deliberate drop in WHO risk drinking level. The Henssler and Witkiewitz data suggest such reductions are clinically meaningful and often sustained, provided he is not severely dependent.
- **Practical levers.** Self-efficacy in refusing drinks predicted success in the Dry January studies. Practising drink refusal at the golf club and using an app or tracker are evidence-linked levers.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT of temporary abstinence. The Dry January evidence is observational and self-reported.
- I found no study of "abstinence then partial return (wine only)" as a structured plan.
- Henssler's subgroup estimates for mild AUD specifically were not reported in the abstract.

## 3. Medicines: naltrexone, acamprosate, nalmefene, topiramate, gabapentin. Should a GP be involved?

### Takeaway
Oral naltrexone (50 mg) and acamprosate are first-line, with modest effects:
- NNT about 11 (acamprosate) and 18 (naltrexone) to prevent a return to any drinking.
- NNT 11 for naltrexone to prevent a return to heavy drinking.

Topiramate has moderate evidence but more side effects. Gabapentin has weaker evidence. Nalmefene (UK/EU, taken "as needed") is licensed to reduce drinking in dependent drinkers with high drinking risk; it cut about 2–3 heavy drinking days a month compared with placebo. All are prescription-only and were tested mainly in moderate–severe AUD. A heavy social drinker should see a GP for screening (AUDIT), liver and blood-pressure checks, and a medication discussion if cutting down fails.

### Cited Findings
- McPheeters et al., JAMA, 7 Nov 2023 (330:1653–1665; 118 RCTs, 20,976 participants, ≥12 weeks):
  - NNT to prevent return to any drinking: acamprosate 11; oral naltrexone 50 mg/day 18.
  - NNT to prevent return to heavy drinking: oral naltrexone 11 (95% CI 5–41).
  - Injectable naltrexone: −4.99 drinking days per 30 days, with no effect on return to any or heavy drinking.
  - Neither acamprosate nor naltrexone was consistently better than the other.
  - Topiramate was the off-label drug with moderate evidence of benefit.
  - Applicability to mild AUD is uncertain, and adverse-effect data were limited.
  — [PubMed 37934220](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37934220/); [The Hospitalist summary](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/index.php/content/most-effective-meds-alcohol-use-disorder-flagged)
- Gabapentin and topiramate have less evidence of efficacy and more potential side effects than the first-line drugs. — [Medicine Today, Dec 2023](https://et.medicinetoday.com.au/mt/2023/december/journal-watch/comparing-pharmacotherapy-options-alcohol-use-disorder)
- Acamprosate and oral naltrexone are effective but underused. — [Boston University AOD Health, Apr 2024](https://www.bu.edu/aodhealth/2024/04/26/acamprosate-and-oral-naltrexone-are-effective-but-underutilized-medications-for-alcohol-use-disorder/)
- **Nalmefene (NICE TA325):**
  - Recommended for alcohol dependence with a high drinking risk level (>60 g/day men, >40 g/day women), no physical withdrawal symptoms, and no need for immediate detox.
  - Taken once a day "as needed" when drinking is anticipated, with continuous psychosocial support.
  - NICE summary: compared with placebo plus support, it reduces heavy drinking days by 3.2 per month and total intake by 1.8 units a day.
  — [NICE TA325](https://nice.org.uk/guidance/ta325/resources/nalmefene-for-reducing-alcohol-consumption-in-people-with-alcohol-dependence-82602488589253); [NICE news](https://www.nice.org.uk/news/article/drug-recommended-to-help-cut-drink-dependence)
- In a 604-patient RCT of as-needed nalmefene 18 mg, heavy drinking days at month 6 fell by −2.3 days/month compared with placebo (95% CI −3.8 to −0.8; p=0.0021). Adverse events and dropouts due to them were more common on nalmefene. — [Heidelberg record of RCT](https://heibib.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/search/Record/1580978304)
- Primary-care brief interventions have a Cochrane evidence base (Kaner et al., Cochrane 2018). It is cited in the Lancet 2026 trial; no effect size was retrieved here. — [Lancet SEMALCO reference list](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(26)00305-3/fulltext)

### Inferences
- **These drugs are not first-line for a social binge pattern.** For a non-dependent heavy social drinker, the medicines are second-line after behavioural change. If he cannot hold the wine-only plan, naltrexone (US/UK/AU) or nalmefene (UK/EU, made for "reduce, not stop" goals and taken before a drinking occasion such as a golf round) fit his pattern best.
- **A GP visit is worthwhile.** Reasons:
  - Baseline liver tests, BP and weight give him measurable markers. Mehta-style improvements motivate.
  - Screening with AUDIT/AUDIT-C picks up dependence that would change the withdrawal plan (section 6).
  - The GP can coordinate with smoking-cessation medicines.
  - Every AUD drug is prescription-only.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes for topiramate (drinking days, heavy drinking days, drinks per drinking day) and gabapentin from McPheeters were not retrieved.
- Specific Cochrane naltrexone and acamprosate reviews were not retrieved in this session.
- Nalmefene availability outside UK/EU (not marketed orally in the US, to my knowledge) was not verified.
- Contraindications were not covered, e.g. naltrexone with opioid use and liver disease. They need a clinical source.

## 4. GLP-1 drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide) for alcohol and smoking

### Takeaway
2025–2026 has brought the first positive RCTs of semaglutide for AUD:
- A small US lab-and-outpatient trial (JAMA Psychiatry, Feb 2025).
- A 108-patient, 26-week Danish RCT in AUD with obesity. Heavy drinking days fell 13.7 percentage points more than placebo (Lancet, May 2026).
- An oral semaglutide trial that missed its primary endpoint but cut heavy drinking days (Am J Psychiatry, Jul 2026).

Large observational studies agree but probably overstate the effect. For smoking, RCTs are mixed or negative: GLP-1s mainly reduce post-quit weight gain and craving, not quit rates. All alcohol and smoking use is off-label. For a man who is overweight, drinks heavily and smokes, a GLP-1 prescribed for weight is a plausible discussion with a doctor. The alcohol benefit is a promising bonus, not an established treatment.

### Cited Findings
**RCTs in alcohol use disorder**
- **Hendershot et al., JAMA Psychiatry, 12 Feb 2025 (82:395–405; NCT05520775; UNC Chapel Hill).** 48 non-treatment-seeking adults with AUD (71% women). Semaglutide 0.25 mg/week for weeks 1–4, 0.5 mg for weeks 5–8, then 1.0 mg; 9 weeks in total.
  - Lab self-administration at 0.5 mg: grams consumed β −0.48 (95% CI −0.85 to −0.11; p=.01); peak breath alcohol β −0.46 (p=.03).
  - Drinks per drinking day: β −0.41 (p=.04).
  - Drinks per calendar day: not significant (p=.17).
  - Weekly craving: β −0.39 (p=.01).
  - Heavy drinking days: significant treatment × time effect (p=.04). The coefficient direction should be checked in the paper.
  - Effect sizes were large (d≥0.80) in weeks 5–8.
  - Smokers subgroup (n=13): cigarettes per day fell more (β −0.10; p=.005).
  - Weight: −5% vs +0.18% with placebo.
  - No serious adverse events and no adverse interactions with alcohol.
  - Caveats: small sample, low doses, short duration. — [PMC11822619](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11822619/)
- **SEMALCO: Klausen, …, Volkow, …, Fink-Jensen, The Lancet, published 2 May 2026 (vol 407:1687–1698; NCT05895643; Copenhagen, single centre).**
  - 108 treatment-seeking adults (53 women, 55 men) with moderate–severe AUD and obesity. Semaglutide 2.4 mg/week subcutaneously or placebo, both with standard CBT, for 26 weeks.
  - Heavy drinking days: −41.1 percentage points from baseline (95% CI −48.7 to −33.5) with semaglutide vs −26.4 (−34.1 to −18.6) with placebo. The estimated treatment difference was −13.7 percentage points (−22.0 to −5.4; p=0.0015).
  - 81% completed the trial. The trial reports "substantial effects on multiple secondary alcohol-related and somatic outcomes".
  - Adverse events were transient, mild to moderate and gastrointestinal, and more frequent on semaglutide.
  - Funders include the Novo Nordisk Foundation. — [The Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(26)00305-3/fulltext)
- **Oral semaglutide (Rybelsus) trial, American Journal of Psychiatry (APA release 29 Jul 2026; NCT05892432; University of Colorado Anschutz).** 50 treatment-seeking adults with moderate–severe AUD, averaging about 6 drinks a day and about 5 heavy drinking days a week. Oral semaglutide 3 mg/day for 4 weeks, then 7 mg/day for 4 weeks.
  - Primary outcome (lab cue-induced craving): not significant.
  - Drinks per day: not significant.
  - Heavy drinking days in the final 4 weeks: significantly reduced.
  - Everyday craving and alcohol consequences: reduced.
  - At least one WHO risk-level drop: 81% vs about 54% with placebo.

- **Strength of evidence.** For alcohol: moderate and rising. There are 3 positive or partly positive semaglutide RCTs (total N about 200), with the strongest result in people with obesity. The trials show less weight gain and craving, not better quit rates.
- **Real option or experimental?** For this user it is a legitimate conversation if he meets weight-loss prescribing criteria (BMI thresholds vary by country). The weight indication is approved, and the alcohol and smoking-weight effects are plausible extras. It should not be started for alcohol alone; that remains experimental and off-label. It should not replace first-line cessation medicines (varenicline, NRT) or AUD medicines.
- **Overlap with smoking cessation.** GLP-1s blunting post-quit weight gain addresses the user's weight worry about stopping smoking. Weight-gain management is likely covered by another research stream.

- Full SEMALCO secondary outcomes were not retrieved: total alcohol, weight change, liver enzymes, smoking.

- The exact journal of the 2026 semaglutide smoking phase 2a is unconfirmed (search summaries say JAMA Network Open).
- No phase 3 GLP-1 AUD trial has been reported. Registry checks for ongoing tirzepatide AUD RCTs were not done.
- No data on GLP-1 drugs specifically in non-dependent heavy social drinkers.

## 5. Alcohol-free / low-alcohol beer as a substitute: help or cue?

### Takeaway
The evidence is mixed and thin:
- **Population purchase data:** no/lo beer mostly substitutes for stronger beer rather than acting as a gateway, but the effect is small.
- **Heavy drinkers:** may use no/lo on top of their usual drinking.
- **People with AUD:** lab studies show craving and physiological cue reactivity after alcohol-free or placebo beer, scaling with dependence severity. The response is driven by belief and sensory cues.

Use to cut down is rising (39–44% of risky drinkers in GB trying to cut down use it), but effectiveness is unproven. No study tests whether alcohol-free beer triggers smoking. Alcohol itself is a strong smoking-relapse trigger.

### Cited Findings
- **Caballeria et al., Nutrients, 22 Sep 2022.** Systematic review of 10 studies of heavy drinkers and people with AUD, from 4,045 records screened.
  - "Craving and desire to drink increase after the consumption of NoLo drinks". The increase correlated with dependence severity (Rho 0.384, N=20, p<0.05; Long et al. 1989), with no increase after a soft-drink control.
  - Former patients: 47% had used low-alcohol drinks since treatment, and 44% of those felt it helped abstinence (self-report).
  - "Heavy drinkers tend to use NoLo drinks on top of their usual alcohol consumption" (Corfe 2020).
  - No prospective clinical study showed successful substitution. The authors advise that "the precautionary principle should prevail". — [PMC9571501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9571501/)
- **Cue-reactivity lab studies in the review:**
  - Kaplan 1983/1984: alcoholics who believed a placebo was real beer showed the highest skin-conductance responses. Belief, not alcohol content, drove reactivity.
  - Dolinsky 1987: 8 dependent inpatients showed larger and faster glucose and insulin responses to placebo beer they believed was alcoholic.
  - Labelling RCTs: people drank more when labels said lower strength (linear trend, n=264), and about 23% more volume when %ABV was shown. — [PMC9571501](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9571501/)
- In recreational drinkers, half a pint of low-alcohol beer increased craving in men but not in women (Southampton). — [Univ. Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/18472)
- 2025 SSA conference abstract (Mollaahmetoglu; 131 risky drinkers): core-branded no/lo adverts did not raise craving compared with non-core-branded ones, and gave less craving than soft-drink adverts. Preliminary. — [SSA 2025](https://www.addiction-ssa.org/events/annual-conference/2025/speakers/merve-mollaahmetoglu/)
- **Buss et al., BMJ Public Health, 23 Sep 2025 (UCL; Smoking and Alcohol Toolkit Study; 9,397 GB adults with AUDIT-C ≥5 trying to cut down).**
  - Use of alcohol-free or low drinks to cut down rose from 35.0% (Oct 2020) to 43.9% (Aug 2024) in serious attempts, and from 25.5% to 38.8% in any attempt.
  - Use of evidence-based support stayed low.
  - The authors call for research "to establish their effectiveness for alcohol reduction". — [BMJ Public Health / UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10211336/1/Brown_e002775.full.pdf)
- **Jané Llopis et al., Alcohol & Alcoholism, May 2022 (64,280 GB households, 2015–2018).**
  - Households new to a brand that bought its no/lo beer were less than a third as likely to later buy the same-brand strong beer (OR 0.320).
  - Among existing buyers, the launch was linked to about 22.5% less parent-beer volume on purchase days.
  - Conclusion: "not a gateway". — [PMC9270994](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9270994)
- **Anderson & Kokole, Nutrients, Oct 2022 (Spain; 18,954 households, 2017–2022).** Each extra 100 ml of zero-alcohol beer was associated with 0.8–1.4 g less alcohol purchased. Lowering the ABV of regular beer had much larger effects (−9.5 to −13.2 g per 1-point ABV cut). The authors call the substitution effect "albeit very small". — [PMC9572185](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9572185)
- Another Spanish household analysis (per search excerpt) found households newly buying zero-alcohol beer cut total alcohol purchases by 5.5% (n=1,791), and by 14% among those who had never bought the same-brand strong beer (n=337). — [SciencePod summary](https://magazine.sciencepod.net/?p=197499) (not fetched; verify)
- Alcohol-free beer in the UK is up to 0.05% ABV, but "in practice some alcohol-free beers contain up to 0.5% ABV". Low-alcohol is up to 1.2%. — [Drinkaware](http://www.drinkaware.co.uk/alcohol-facts/alcoholic-drinks-units/lager)
- **Alcohol as a smoking trigger:**
  - A 2023 systematic review found alcohol use linked to lower quit success in 20 of 27 studies. In 12 of 13 experimental studies, alcohol or alcohol cues increased the urge to smoke. — [Amsterdam UMC PDF](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/files/168253300/The-effect-of-alcohol-use-on-smoking-cessation.pdf)
  - Among heavy drinkers in cessation treatment, moderate drinking days carried about 4× the lapse risk of non-drinking days. — [PMC2893295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2893295)

### Inferences
- **Mild dependence: probably a useful swap.** For a non-dependent social drinker, an alcohol-free beer at the 19th hole can be a socially easy substitute. The purchase data suggest substitution rather than escalation.
- **Higher dependence or AUD: a cue risk.** If his AUDIT score suggests dependence or he has a history of losing control, the AUD cue-reactivity data argue for caution.
- **His plan conflicts with alcohol-free beer.** His plan keeps beer off permanently. Alcohol-free beer keeps the beer ritual (taste, glass, bar, golf-club context) alive, and that ritual is also linked to smoking. A practical test: if alcohol-free beer brings cravings for real beer or a cigarette, switch to non-beer drinks. This is inferred from the cue literature; no trial tested it.
- **Weeks 1–2 are high risk for smoking relapse.** If he quits smoking at the same time, abstaining from alcohol in the first weeks addresses a documented relapse trigger.

### Gaps
- No study was found on whether alcohol-free beer specifically triggers smoking urges.
- No RCT measures actual consumption (rather than purchases) after substituting alcohol-free beer in heavy social drinkers.
- No direct head-to-head comparison of alcohol-free beer cues and regular beer cues in non-dependent drinkers.
- Calories in alcohol-free beer were not sourced.

## 6. Alcohol withdrawal safety when stopping suddenly

### Takeaway
Most heavy social or weekend drinkers can stop abruptly without danger. People who are physically dependent should not stop suddenly without medical advice. Signs of dependence are daily heavy drinking, morning shakes, sweats or nausea, drinking to relieve symptoms, and past withdrawal.

NICE thresholds:
- More than 15 UK units a day, or AUDIT ≥20: offer assisted withdrawal, usually in the community.
- More than 30 units a day, SADQ >30, or a history of withdrawal seizures or DTs: inpatient or residential withdrawal.

UK AUDIT-C score of 11–12 is the "possible dependence" band and should prompt a GP check before day 1 (26 Oct).

### Cited Findings
- NICE CG115 (UK, 2011):
  - Offer structured assisted withdrawal, community-based unless there are safety concerns, to people who drink >15 units a day or score ≥20 on AUDIT.
  - Offer inpatient or residential withdrawal to people who drink >30 units a day, score >30 on SADQ, have a history of epilepsy, withdrawal seizures or delirium tremens, need concurrent benzodiazepine withdrawal, or are homeless.
  - Also offer it to people drinking 15–20 units a day with significant psychiatric or physical comorbidity or cognitive impairment.
  - Adjust the criteria for women, older people and those with liver disease.
  - Tools: SADQ or LDQ for dependence; CIWA-Ar for withdrawal severity. — [NICE CG115 news](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg115/documents/people-who-misuse-alcohol-to-benefit-from-new-guidance); [NICE CG115 key priorities](https://nice.org.uk/guidance/cg115/chapter/Key-priorities-for-implementation); [PMC3223772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3223772)
- PAWSS (Prediction of Alcohol Withdrawal Severity Scale) ≥4 means high risk of complicated withdrawal, and admission should be considered.
- CIWA-Ar bands:
  - <8: may not need detox.
  - 8–15: outpatient detox if no DTs or seizures.
  - ≥15: admit.
- A history of withdrawal seizures or DTs makes outpatient management inappropriate even when symptoms are mild. A pulse >100 bpm is an additional risk sign. — [The Hospitalist](https://blogs.the-hospitalist.org/content/should-patient-who-requests-alcohol-detoxification-be-admitted-or-treated-outpatient); [Stanford PAWSS trial page](https://clinicaltrials.stanford.edu/trials/t/NCT01637415.html)
- NHS-affiliated guidance: "Do not suddenly stop drinking if you are dependent on alcohol. If you are physically dependent on alcohol, stopping suddenly can be harmful and may cause serious withdrawal symptoms."
  - Warning signs: anxiety on waking, sweating or shaking, morning nausea or retching, vomiting, hallucinations, seizures. Hallucinations and seizures need urgent help (999/A&E). — [NHS Wales GP practice page](https://www.nantgarwroadmedicalcentre.wales.nhs.uk/acmhainn-slinte/27176-alcohol-support); [Sherwood Forest Hospitals leaflet](https://sfh-tr.nhs.uk/media/liontmlf/pil202505-06-hrais-how-to-reduce-your-alcohol-intake-safely.pdf); [SWBH leaflet](https://www.swbh.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2012/07/Alcohol-dependence-ML5608.pdf)
- Detox settings range from a home reduction plan without medication for mild withdrawal, to home detox with medication (usually 8–10 days), to hospital or residential care for severe dependence. — [Torbay & South Devon NHS](https://www.torbayandsouthdevon.nhs.uk/services/drug-and-alcohol-service/detoxification/)
- UK AUDIT-C bands: 0–4 lower risk; 5–7 increasing risk; 8–10 higher risk; 11–12 possible dependence. A score of 5 or more is a positive screen. A score is not a diagnosis. — [Hampshire County Council guidance](https://documents.hants.gov.uk/public-health/alcohol/Intervention-Guidance.docx); [Drinkaware AUDIT](https://www.drinkaware.co.uk/facts/information-about-alcohol/alcohol-and-the-facts/understanding-the-alcohol-use-disorders-identification-test)
- Nalmefene's licence excludes people with physical withdrawal symptoms or needing immediate detox. That marks the line between "reduce" candidates and "detox first" patients. — [NICE TA325](https://nice.org.uk/guidance/ta325/resources/nalmefene-for-reducing-alcohol-consumption-in-people-with-alcohol-dependence-82602488589253)

### Inferences
- **Self-check before 26 Oct.** Does he drink every day? Does he get shakes, sweats, nausea or anxiety on mornings without a drink? Has he ever had a fit or hallucinations when stopping? Does he drink more than about 15 UK units (roughly 6–7 pints of 4% lager) on a typical day? Is his AUDIT-C 11 or more?
  - If all answers are no and the drinking is episodic (golf days, weekends), stopping abruptly for two weeks is low risk.
  - If any answer is yes, he should see a GP before day 1. The pint conversion is from Drinkaware's 2.6 units for a 4.6% pint.
- **Mild symptoms can still occur.** Even non-dependent heavy drinkers may notice poorer sleep, irritability or anxiety in the first days. Hallucinations, confusion or a seizure are emergencies.
- **Smoking cessation adds overlap.** Nicotine withdrawal can overlap with alcohol-related irritability and poor sleep in the first two weeks. This is a planning point, not a safety threshold.

### Gaps
- ASAM 2020 withdrawal guideline thresholds and NIAAA Core Resource outpatient criteria were not retrieved.
- No validated AUDIT-C cut-off specifically predicts withdrawal severity. AUDIT-C screens for risk and dependence, not withdrawal.
- No US or Australian equivalents of the NICE unit thresholds were retrieved.
- The main NHS.uk alcohol-withdrawal page was not retrieved; the NHS citations are regional NHS pages.

## 7. Alcohol and weight: calories in beer and the effect of stopping

### Takeaway
Alcohol has about 7 kcal per gram. A pint of 4% lager is about 180 kcal and a pint of 5% beer about 215 kcal. One month off alcohol cut weight by a median 1.5% (81.1 → 79.5 kg) in Mehta 2018, and 54% of Dry January participants reported losing weight. The NASEM review drew no firm conclusion on moderate drinking and weight. Stopping beer removes many calories, but compensatory eating can offset this.

### Cited Findings
- Alcohol "contains around seven calories a gram – almost as many as a gram of fat". A pint of 4% lager has about 180 kcal; a 4.6% pint has 2.6 units. — [Drinkaware](http://www.drinkaware.co.uk/alcohol-facts/alcoholic-drinks-units/lager)
- A pint of 5% beer has 215 kcal according to an NHS-style table. Other sources give about 200–239 kcal for 4.5–5% pints. — [EANI HealthWell](https://healthwell.eani.org.uk/node/3224); [Hukins Hops](https://www.hukins-hops.co.uk/our-story/news-press/how-many-calories-in-a-pint-of-lager/) (brewer blog, lower quality)
- Mehta 2018 (1 month abstinent): weight −1.5% (IQR −2.9% to −0.4%), median 81.1 → 79.5 kg; control group p=0.09 (no significant change). There were also falls in insulin resistance (−25.9%) and cholesterol (−13.4%). — [PMC5942469](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5942469)
- In de Visser's 2019 Dry January evaluation, 54% self-reported weight loss. — [Alcohol Change UK](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/help-and-support/managing-your-drinking/dry-january/about-dry-january/the-evidence-behind-the-dry-january-challenge-benefits-and-long-term-impact)
- NASEM 2024 on weight:
  - Men: changes in BMI and obesity risk are similar across moderate intake levels (low certainty).
  - Women: evidence is inconsistent.
  - No conclusion compared with never drinking. — [National Academies](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/2024/12/new-report-reviews-evidence-on-moderate-alcohol-consumption-and-health-impacts)
- In GLP-1 trials, weight fell about 5% over 9–10 weeks on semaglutide vs +0.18% to +1.58% on placebo (Hendershot 2025; 2026 smoking trial). — [PMC11822619](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11822619/); [ReachMD](https://reachmd.com/news/once-weekly-semaglutide-for-daily-cigarette-use-trial-results/2487436/)

### Inferences
- **Illustrative arithmetic, not from a study.** A golf day of 5 pints of 4% lager is about 900 kcal (5 × ~180). Two such days a week is about 1,800 kcal a week. Replacing that with 2–3 glasses of wine is a large net calorie cut, before counting the bar snacks that go with beer.
- **Smoking cessation pulls the other way.** Quitting smoking tends to cause weight gain (covered elsewhere). Cutting beer calories could partly offset this, which is a useful framing for this user.

### Gaps
- No calorie figure was sourced for a glass of wine or for alcohol-free beer.
- No US NIAAA figure (12 oz regular beer) was retrieved.
- No RCT measured weight change after stopping alcohol over more than 1 month in heavy drinkers. No data quantify compensatory eating after quitting alcohol.

## 8. What is genuinely new in 2024–2026?

### Takeaway
The main developments, in order:
- **Policy divergence.** The US Surgeon General's cancer advisory (Jan 2025) and the ICCPUD draft (Jan 2025) moved toward "less is better, risk starts low". The US then shelved the ICCPUD report (Sep 2025) and dropped numeric limits (DGA, Jan 2026).
- **First positive semaglutide RCTs for AUD**, culminating in the Lancet SEMALCO trial (May 2026).
- **Observational GLP-1 data** in Sweden (2024) and the US (Truveta 2026) that agree but carry confounding caveats.
- **Mixed or negative GLP-1 smoking RCTs.**
- **Rising use of no/lo drinks to cut down** without proof of effectiveness (BMJ Public Health 2025).
