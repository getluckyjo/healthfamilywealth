# Behavioural support, financial incentives, and quitting smoking and alcohol together (evidence as of October 2026)

Context for the report writer: these notes are for a man who has relapsed to smoking and drinks too much beer socially at the golf course, where beer and cigarettes go together. His plan is to stop smoking and alcohol on Mon 26 Oct 2026, with no alcohol for 2 weeks and then an occasional glass of wine with his wife. Effect sizes are relative unless stated. RR = risk ratio, OR = odds ratio, CI = confidence interval, CrI = credible interval. "Minimal support" usually means a leaflet, brief advice or usual care.

## 1. Behavioural support: effect sizes for quitlines, individual and group counselling, and medication combined with behavioural support

### Takeaway
Every main form of behavioural support adds to quit rates. Counselling raises the odds of quitting by about 44% over minimal support (high certainty). Combining behavioural support with stop-smoking medication raises quit rates by about 83% over usual care or brief advice, and this combination is the best-supported approach. Behavioural support still adds about 10–20% when the person is already taking medication. Quitlines help (RR about 1.25–1.38), and group counselling is about as effective as individual counselling.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane overview and network meta-analysis (Hartmann-Boyce et al., Cochrane Database Syst Rev, published 4 Jan 2021, CD013229.pub2).** It covered 33 Cochrane reviews, 312 RCTs and 250,563 participants, with abstinence measured at 6 months or more.
  - High-certainty benefit for counselling: OR 1.44 (95% CrI 1.22–1.70; 194 studies, n=72,273).
  - High-certainty benefit for guaranteed financial incentives: OR 1.46 (95% CrI 1.15–1.85; 19 studies, n=8,877).
  - Moderate-certainty benefit for text-message delivery, individual tailoring, motivational content and content focused on "how to quit".
  - More contacts, longer contacts and longer programmes had point estimates showing modest benefit, but the CrIs included no difference.
  - The effect of behavioural support was "slightly less pronounced" when people were also taking medication, but it still added benefit. No evidence of harm was found.
  - Note: the network meta-analysis reports odds ratios, not risk ratios.
  - Source: [Hartmann-Boyce 2021, PubMed 33411338](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33411338/)
- ASH's plain-language summary of the same review says counselling increases quit success "by 44%" and financial incentives "by 46%" compared with no support. It adds that "People's best chances of quitting are using a licensed stop smoking medication and behavioural support at the same time." The ASH briefing is dated Feb 2021 — [ASH explainer](https://ash.org.uk/uploads/The-Cochrane-Review-of-behavioural-interventions-for-smoking-cessation-explained.pdf)
- A follow-up component network meta-analysis (Addiction, 2022) reported, per its repository abstract:
  - Text-message support beat telephone (OR 1.48, 95% CrI 1.13–1.94) and print materials (OR 1.44, 1.14–1.83).
  - Individual delivery was less effective than group delivery (OR 0.78, 0.64–0.95).
  - I saw only the abstract summary, not the full text — [Univ. of Leicester repository](https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/Behavioural_programmes_for_cigarette_smoking_cessation_investigating_interactions_between_behavioural_motivational_and_delivery_components_in_a_systematic_review_and_component_network_meta-analysis/19175060)
- **Quitlines and telephone counselling (Matkin et al., Cochrane, 2 May 2019; 104 trials, n=111,653).**
  - People who called a quitline: multiple sessions of proactive counselling increased quitting vs self-help or a single call (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.19–1.61; 14 trials, n=32,484; moderate certainty).
  - Smokers who had not called a quitline: telephone counselling RR 1.25 (1.15–1.35; 65 trials, n=41,233; moderate certainty).
  - Offering 3–5 calls rather than 1 call: RR 1.27 (1.12–1.44).
  - Telephone support worked better for people motivated to quit.
  - Source: [Matkin 2019, PubMed 31045250](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31045250/)
- **Individual counselling (Lancaster & Stead, Cochrane, 31 Mar 2017; 49 trials, about 19,000 participants).**
  - Without medication: individual counselling vs minimal contact RR 1.57 (1.40–1.77; 27 studies; high quality).
  - When everyone also received NRT: RR 1.24 (1.01–1.51; moderate).
  - More intensive vs brief counselling: RR 1.29 (1.09–1.53).
  - Source: [Lancaster & Stead 2017, PubMed 28361496](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28361496/)
- **Group therapy (Stead, Carroll & Lancaster, Cochrane, 31 Mar 2017; 66 trials).**
  - Group programme vs self-help: RR 1.88 (1.52–2.33; moderate).
  - Group vs brief support from a clinician: RR 1.22 (1.03–1.43; low).
  - Group vs no intervention: RR 2.60 (1.80–3.76; low).
  - Group vs individual counselling of similar intensity: RR 0.99 (0.76–1.28), so no difference.
  - Source: [Stead 2017, PubMed 28361497](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28361497/)
- **Combined medication and behavioural support (Stead et al., Cochrane, 24 Mar 2016; 52 studies, n=19,488).**
  - Combination vs usual care, brief advice or less intensive support: RR 1.83 (1.68–1.98; high quality).
  - The effect was larger in healthcare settings (RR 1.97) than in the community (RR 1.53).
  - Indirect comparisons did not show that more intensive support gave larger effects.
  - Source: [Stead 2016, PubMed 27009521](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27009521/)
- **Extra behavioural support for people already on medication (Hartmann-Boyce et al., Cochrane, 5 Jun 2019; 83 studies, n=29,536).**
  - Pooled RR 1.15 (1.08–1.22; 65 studies, n=23,331; high certainty).
  - The authors' summary: more behavioural support "is likely to increase the chance of success by about 10% to 20%".
  - Source: [Hartmann-Boyce 2019, PubMed 31166007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31166007/)
- **NHS consumer messaging (UK).** The NHS Better Health page says that with "professional or expert help, you're 3 times more likely to quit for good" — [NHS Better Health – local stop smoking service](https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/quit-smoking/find-your-local-stop-smoking-service/). This "3 times" figure is a marketing claim. It is larger than any single pooled Cochrane estimate above, and it likely compares service users who also took medication against unaided quitters.

### Inferences
- For a relapsed smoker, the evidence points to the same "stack" each time: medication, plus structured behavioural support (a stop-smoking service, quitline or counsellor), plus extra layers such as text messages or incentives. Each layer gives a modest relative gain, and the gains appear roughly additive (the network meta-analysis assumed additivity).
- The format can follow his preference. Group, individual and telephone support give broadly similar effects. The main requirement is several contacts spread over the first weeks.

### Gaps
- I found no updated Cochrane behavioural overview after 2021. The 2017 individual and group counselling reviews and the 2019 telephone review have not, as far as I found, been updated by 2026.
- NICE NG209's recommendation text could not be retrieved (HTTP 403 and a redirect), so I could not quote its current wording here.

## 2. Digital support: text messaging, smartphone apps and AI or chatbot coaching

### Takeaway
Automated text-message programmes have moderate-certainty evidence of benefit (RR about 1.5). Evidence on apps has gone from "no proven benefit" (Cochrane 2019) to "probably helps, low certainty" (2026 meta-analysis). The best single trial is iCanQuit, an acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) app: 28.2% vs 21.1% abstinent at 12 months. AI chatbots do not yet have proven benefit. The fully powered QuitBot RCT posted null 12-month results in May 2026 (22.7% vs 23.1% for SmokefreeTXT), and a 2026 systematic review found no RCTs of large-language-model (LLM) chatbots up to Jan 2025.

### Cited Findings
- **Text messaging and apps (Whittaker et al., Cochrane, 22 Oct 2019; 26 studies, n=33,849; searches to Oct 2018).**
  - Automated text messaging vs minimal support: RR 1.54 (1.19–2.00; 13 studies, n=14,133; moderate certainty).
  - Text messaging added to other support vs that support alone: RR 1.59 (1.09–2.33; 4 studies, n=997).
  - High- vs low-intensity messaging: RR 1.00.
  - Smartphone apps vs lower-intensity support: RR 1.00 (0.66–1.52; 5 studies, n=3,079; very low certainty).
  - Source: [Whittaker 2019, PubMed 31638271](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31638271/)
- I found no Cochrane update of this review after 2019 — [Cochrane summary page](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD006611_can-programmes-delivered-mobile-phones-help-people-stop-smoking)
- **iCanQuit (Bricker et al., JAMA Intern Med, published online 21 Sep 2020; Nov 2020 issue).**
  - Double-blind RCT of 2,415 US adults comparing the ACT-based iCanQuit app with NCI's QuitGuide app, which follows US clinical guidelines.
  - 30-day point-prevalence abstinence at 12 months: 28.2% (293/1040) vs 21.1% (225/1067), OR 1.49 (95% CI 1.22–1.83). Retention was 87.2%.
  - iCanQuit teaches acceptance of triggers. QuitGuide teaches avoidance of triggers.
  - Source: [Bricker 2020, PubMed 32955554](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32955554/)
  - An AAFP summary puts this as about 1 extra quitter for every 14 users — [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2021/0401/p441)
- **Smartphone app meta-analysis (Chu et al., BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine, published 21 Jul 2026; 31 RCTs, n=12,802; searches to Aug 2025).**
  - Apps alone vs no or minimal support, 6-month continuous abstinence: RR 2.85 (1.61–5.05; 4 studies; low certainty).
  - Apps plus traditional treatment vs traditional treatment alone: RR 1.98 (1.24–3.16; I²=86%; low certainty).
  - Apps plus medication vs medication alone: RR 1.77 (1.09–2.88; low certainty).
  - Apps based on process-based therapies, such as ACT, vs traditional behavioural apps, 7-day abstinence at 6 months: RR 1.36 (1.19–1.54; 4 studies, n=3,258; high certainty).
  - The authors call the findings "exploratory".
  - Source: [Chu 2026, PubMed 41529951](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41529951/)
- **e-health meta-analysis (Li et al., EClinicalMedicine, Jan 2024; 39 articles, n=17,351).**
  - e-health vs "traditional" interventions, sustained long-term quitting: RR 1.79 (1.60–2.00).
  - Text and telephone, short-term quitting: RR 2.10. Web and app, short-term quitting: RR 1.74.
  - The comparators varied and the effects look larger than the Cochrane estimates, so treat this with caution.
  - Source: [Li 2024, PubMed 38273889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38273889/)
- **Quit Genius RCT (Webb et al., JMIR Mental Health, Oct 2020; UK; n=556 randomised, 530 in the intention-to-treat analysis).**
  - Quit Genius, a CBT-based digital therapeutic app, vs very brief advice.
  - 7-day abstinence at 4 weeks after the quit date: 44.5% vs 28.7%, RR 1.55 (1.23–1.96). Self-report agreed with CO readings in 96% of available cases.
  - Short-term outcome only.
  - Source: [Webb 2020, PubMed 33021488](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33021488/)
- **Smoke Free app, RAUCHFREI trial (Keller et al., preprint 19 Mar 2026; Germany).**
  - Remote double-blind RCT, n=1,466 moderately dependent smokers. Both arms received brief advice from a healthcare professional.
  - Self-reported 7-day abstinence at 6 months: 39.3% vs 24.4% for a text-only information app, OR 2.01 (1.60–2.50).
  - Funded by the app maker (Smoke Free 23 GmbH). Not yet peer reviewed.
  - Source: [Keller 2026 preprint, DOI 10.64898/2026.03.17.26348617](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.17.26348617)
- The NHS Quit Smoking app is described by the NHS as offering "personalised support", with progress tracking, savings and daily support. I found no RCT of the NHS app itself — [NHS 10 self-help tips](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/quit-smoking/10-self-help-tips-to-stop-smoking/)
- **QuitBot pilot (Bricker et al., JMIR mHealth uHealth, 23 Jul 2024).**
  - Among people who viewed all 42 days of content, 30-day abstinence at 3 months was 63% (39/62) vs 38.5% (45/117) for SmokefreeTXT (OR 2.58, 1.34–4.99).
  - This was a complete-case subgroup in a pilot, so it is prone to bias.
  - Source: [Bricker 2024, PubMed 38913882](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38913882/)
- **QuitBot full-scale RCT (NCT04308759).**
  - Design: n=1,520 planned, compared with SmokefreeTXT, with 12-month follow-up. The chatbot uses an "expert-curated large language model" for open-ended questions — [protocol, Contemp Clin Trials Oct 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39490766/)
  - Results posted on ClinicalTrials.gov on 22 May 2026:
    - 30-day abstinence at 12 months: 186/821 (QuitBot) vs 191/826 (SmokefreeTXT), OR 0.96 (95% CI 0.76–1.21), p=0.72.
    - At 6 months: 153/821 vs 159/826, OR 0.92 (0.71–1.19).
    - Source: [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04308759](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04308759)
  - I found no peer-reviewed results paper yet.
- **Systematic review of AI in alcohol and smoking interventions (Harrison et al., preprint 8 Jul 2026; searches to 14 Jan 2025).**
  - 12 smoking RCTs (n=68,659): 8 apps or messenger chatbots and 4 recommender systems. All had "mixed evidence" and most were at high risk of bias.
  - "There were no trials using large language models"; "no strong evidence of a beneficial effect of AI-assistance".
  - Source: [Harrison 2026 preprint, Research Square](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10117247/v1)
- **Other 2026 work.** LLM chatbots for quitting are still at the stage of development and qualitative acceptability studies, e.g. a Nicotine Tob Res study published May 2026 with 21 African American smokers — [McKinney 2026, PubMed 41543124](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41543124/)

### Inferences
- Texting is cheap, has moderate-certainty evidence, and adds to other support (RR 1.59 when added). Free national programmes include SmokefreeTXT in the US and the NHS app or texts in the UK. An evidence-backed app such as iCanQuit or Smoke Free is a reasonable extra layer. ACT-style apps, which teach accepting urges rather than avoiding triggers, have the strongest app evidence.
- So far, AI chatbots perform about the same as a good text-message programme, not better. Using a general-purpose AI assistant as a quit coach has no trial evidence.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed publication of the QuitBot 12-month results was found. The registry data are the only source.
- No RCTs of LLM-based (generative AI) quit coaches were found through early 2025. If any were published between mid-2025 and Oct 2026, I did not locate them.
- No independent RCT of the NHS Quit Smoking app was found. The Quit Genius evidence is short term (4 weeks).

## 3. Financial incentives and commitment (deposit) contracts, including self-applied ones

### Takeaway
The Cochrane review updated in January 2025 gives high-certainty evidence that incentives work: RR 1.52 for quitting at 6 months or more. The effect persists after the incentives stop. The size of the incentive does not clearly matter, and self-funded deposit schemes were not clearly different from paid rewards. Deposit contracts, where you stake your own money, are taken up far less often (about 11–14% of people offered), but they work well for those who sign up. Halpern's NEJM 2015 trial estimated deposit contracts were 13.2 percentage points more effective than rewards among people willing to accept either. A person can apply this to himself, but there are no trials of fully self-run contracts without independent checks.

### Cited Findings
- **Cochrane review (Notley et al., "Incentives for smoking cessation", published 13 Jan 2025, CD004307.pub7; searches to Nov 2023).**
  - Mixed populations: 48 studies, more than 21,924 participants.
  - Quitting at 6 months or longer: RR 1.52 (95% CI 1.33–1.74; I²=23%; 39 studies, n=18,303; high certainty).
  - Excluding studies that were still paying incentives at final follow-up: RR 1.46 (1.23–1.73). The effect "is sustained even when the last follow-up occurs after the withdrawal of incentives".
  - Adjusted analysis including cluster RCTs: OR 1.57 (1.37–1.79; 43 studies, n=23,960).
  - Incentive totals ranged from zero (self-deposits) to US$1,185. There was no clear difference between low- and high-value incentives, "nor those encouraging redeemable self-deposits". Meta-regression on value: p=0.963.
  - Pregnancy: RR 2.13 (1.58–2.86), now high certainty.
  - Source: [Notley 2025, PubMed 39799985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39799985/)
- The 2021 Cochrane overview found guaranteed financial incentives OR 1.46 (1.15–1.85), high certainty — [Hartmann-Boyce 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33411338/)
- **Halpern et al., NEJM, 13 May 2015 (CVS Caremark employees, friends and family; n=2,538).**
  - Arms: rewards of about $800, or a $150 refundable deposit plus $650 in rewards, each as an individual or group programme, vs usual care.
  - Acceptance: 90.0% for rewards vs 13.7% for deposits.
  - Sustained abstinence through 6 months (intention to treat): 9.4–16.0% across the incentive arms vs 6.0% with usual care. Rewards beat deposits overall (15.7% vs 10.2%).
  - Among the roughly 13.7% who would accept either programme (instrumental-variable analysis), deposits gave 13.2 percentage points (3.1–22.8) higher abstinence than rewards.
  - Group-based and individual programmes gave similar results (13.7% vs 12.1%).
  - Source: [Halpern 2015, PubMed 25970009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25970009/)
  - Among those who accepted deposit contracts, 52.3% achieved sustained abstinence vs 17.1% of reward acceptors. This comparison is not randomised because acceptance differed — [Pharmaceutical Journal](https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/paying-people-to-quit-smoking-promotes-sustained-abstinence)
- **Halpern et al., NEJM, 23 May 2018 (pragmatic trial in employers, n=6,006, mostly unengaged smokers).**
  - Sustained abstinence at 6 months: 0.1% usual care, 0.5% free cessation aids, 1.0% free e-cigarettes, 2.0% rewards, 2.9% redeemable deposit.
  - Redeemable deposits beat free aids (p<0.001) and free e-cigarettes (p=0.008).
  - Source: [Halpern 2018, PubMed 29791259](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29791259/)
- **CARES commitment savings account (Giné, Karlan & Zinman, AEJ: Applied Economics 2010; Philippines; about 2,000 smokers).**
  - Smokers deposited their own money for 6 months. A urine test for nicotine and cotinine returned the money on a pass, and on a fail it went to charity.
  - 11% of those offered took it up.
  - Those offered were about 3 percentage points more likely to pass at 6 months, and the effect persisted on surprise tests at 12 months.
  - Source: [IPA summary](https://poverty-action.org/publication/put-your-money-where-your-butt)
  - The working-paper version discloses Karlan's and Zinman's ties to stickK.com, a commercial commitment-contract website — [NBER working paper](https://conference.nber.org/confer/2008/si2008/HC/zinman.pdf)
- **Self-tailored online deposit contracts (Jarvis & Dallery, J Appl Behav Anal, 2017; two small experiments, n=9 and n=10).**
  - Participants chose their own deposit (mean $82). CO-verified negative samples rose from 1% at baseline to 47% during treatment.
  - Feasibility only.
  - Source: [Jarvis & Dallery 2017, PubMed 28211949](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28211949/)
- **Combined incentive plus deposit RCT (Anderson et al., J Smok Cessat, 2021; Medicaid enrollees).** Effects were positive but imprecise, e.g. 2-month OR 1.9–2.0 with CIs crossing 1 — [Anderson 2021, PubMed 34306224](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34306224/)
- **Indirect evidence on uptake (physical activity, not smoking).** In an app RCT, uptake was 62% for deposit contracts vs 100% for rewards, and deposits were not more effective than rewards — [de Buisonjé 2022, PubMed 36201384](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36201384/)

### Inferences
- **Can he apply this to himself? Plausibly yes.** Trial deposit contracts share three features: his own money at stake, a fixed date, and loss to a third party (charity) on failure. He can copy all three, for example with a stickK-type contract, a referee, and money that goes to a charity or "anti-charity" if he fails. Those who accept deposit contracts do well.
- **The weak point of self-run contracts is verification.** Trials used CO breath or cotinine tests. A home cotinine strip or CO monitor, checked by a referee such as his wife or a golf partner, would mirror the trial design. This is my inference; no trial tested this exact setup.
- **The Halpern 2015 group arms did not beat individual arms.** So a "golf-buddy pool" would not be expected to add much beyond the individual stake. That trial's evidence on social pressure is null.
- Because the Cochrane meta-regression found no relationship with incentive value, the stake does not need to be large to help. But Cochrane did not test self-set stakes as a separate comparison.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests a fully self-administered contract without researcher verification, such as stickK on its own, for smoking. Outcome data from stickK users are not independently trial-validated.
- The Cochrane 2025 abstract does not give a separate pooled estimate for deposit-only trials. It only says there was "no clear difference".

## 4. Quitting smoking and alcohol at the same time: does it help or hurt?

### Takeaway
The evidence favours cutting alcohol during a quit attempt.
- Drinking is one of the strongest triggers for a lapse. In heavy-drinking smokers, about 42% of first lapses happened while drinking. A drinking day carried about 5 times the lapse risk of a non-drinking day, and even moderate drinking about 4 times.
- Heavy drinkers quit less often.
- Adding alcohol-reduction support to stop-smoking treatment improved quit rates in two RCTs (aOR 1.56 in Kahler 2008; 13.5% vs 10.3% in a quitline trial).
- Quitting smoking does not raise drinking. Most studies show drinking stays the same or falls.
- Smoking treatment during alcohol or drug treatment does not harm, and may help, sobriety (+25% long-term abstinence in Prochaska's 2004 meta-analysis; RR 0.97 for no difference in Cochrane 2016).
- US federal guidance (smokefree.gov) advises staying away from alcohol in the first few weeks.

His plan matches the evidence-based recommendation of a 2023 systematic review: "stop or substantially reduce" alcohol before and/or during the quit attempt.

### Cited Findings
**Alcohol as a lapse and relapse trigger**
- **Kahler, Spillane & Metrik, Nicotine Tob Res 2010.** Heavy-drinking smokers in a cessation RCT, n=236, all on NRT.
  - 41.5% of those who lapsed were drinking at the time of their first lapse. 67.0% drank on the same day as the lapse.
  - Hazard of lapse on drinking vs non-drinking days: HR 5.08. Moderate drinking vs none: HR 3.97. Heavy vs moderate: HR 2.15. Heavy vs none: HR 8.52.
  - Settings of alcohol-involved lapses vs other lapses:
    - bar or restaurant: 39.2% vs 2.9%
    - others smoking: 83.6% vs 42.6%
    - friends present: 64.9% vs 24.0%
    - social function: 39.2% vs 4.8%
    - happy or good mood: 60.8% vs 26.0%
    - at home: 23.0% vs 53.8%
  - The authors advise stressing the lapse risk of "any alcohol consumption" during a quit attempt.
  - The paper also cites older studies (Baer & Lichtenstein 1988, Borland 1990, Shiffman 1982) showing about one quarter of initial lapses occur in alcohol-involved contexts.
  - Source: [Kahler 2010, PMC2893295](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2893295)
- **van Amsterdam & van den Brink, "The effect of alcohol use on smoking cessation: a systematic review", Alcohol, vol. 109, Jun 2023.** No meta-analysis was possible, so the authors used consistency across studies.
  - Alcohol use was linked to lower quit success in 20 of 27 studies. Abstinence was shorter with higher drinking in 19 of 20 long-term follow-up studies. In 12 of 13 experimental studies, alcohol or alcohol cues increased the urge to smoke.
  - Heavy drinking (5+ drinks a day) was linked to roughly double the risk of relapse.
  - In a naltrexone RCT in heavy-drinking smokers, 34% of all smoking lapses occurred while drinking.
  - In one 12-week study, the chance of a lapse was 38.5% on heavy-drinking days, 24.5% on moderate-drinking days and 19.1% on abstinent days.
  - In one EMA study, drinking was associated with lapse at OR 6.99 (3.12–15.66).
  - Even moderate drinkers were less able to resist smoking after alcohol than after a placebo drink (McKee 2006).
  - The interaction is "not reciprocal": cigarettes cue drinking much less than drinking cues cigarettes.
  - Some studies found weak or no effects, including Shiffman et al. 1996, which used a short follow-up.
  - Recommendation: smokers who drink should "stop or substantially reduce their alcohol consumption before and/or during their attempt to quit smoking".
  - Source: [van Amsterdam 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003)

**Heavy drinkers have lower quit rates**
- **ITC Four Country Survey (Kahler et al., Drug Alcohol Depend 2010; n=3,614; Australia, Canada, UK, US; 2-year follow-up).** Smokers who drank heavily (5+ drinks for men) more than once a week "had especially low rates of quitting smoking" — [Kahler 2010 ITC, PubMed 20227840](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20227840/)
- **Toll et al. quitline RCT** (below). Regardless of study arm, callers who reported no heavy drinking were more likely to quit than those reporting any heavy drinking (OR 1.87, 1.29–2.71) — [Toll 2015, PubMed 25419583](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25419583/)
- **Hughes & Kalman 2006 (as summarised in van Amsterdam 2023).** Smokers with past, but not current, alcohol problems were as able to quit on a given attempt as those who never had alcohol problems. This suggests it is current drinking that matters — [van Amsterdam 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003)

**Does cutting alcohol during the quit attempt improve smoking abstinence?**
- **Kahler et al., J Consult Clin Psychol, Oct 2008.** RCT of heavy-drinking smokers, n=236, all on 8 weeks of NRT.
  - Standard 4-session treatment with a built-in brief alcohol intervention, vs standard treatment of equal intensity.
  - The alcohol arm drank about 20% fewer drinks per week and had more smoking abstinence over 26 weeks (aOR 1.56, 95% CI 1.01–2.43).
  - The smoking effect was mainly seen 2 weeks after the quit date and was "essentially absent by 16 weeks". It was stronger in moderately heavy drinkers than in very heavy drinkers.
  - Source: [Kahler 2008, PubMed 18837602](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18837602/)
- **Toll et al., J Consult Clin Psychol (online Nov 2014; 2015 issue).** New York State Smokers' Quitline RCT of hazardous-drinking callers, n=1,948.
  - Brief motivational counselling to limit or stop alcohol, plus a booklet, added to standard quitline care.
  - Smoking abstinence at 7 months: 13.5% vs 10.3% for tobacco-only counselling (intention to treat, p=0.03; respondents: 26.2% vs 20.4%).
  - van Amsterdam reports this as OR 1.37 (1.04–1.80).
  - Source: [Toll 2015, PubMed 25419583](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25419583/)
- **Other integrated trials (summarised in van Amsterdam 2023).**
  - Ames et al. 2014: integrated smoking and alcohol treatment gave 23% vs 11% smoking abstinence at 24 weeks compared with nicotine patch alone.
  - Correa-Fernández et al. 2017 did not confirm a benefit.
  - A web-intervention study found that adding alcohol cessation reduced drinking-induced lapses.
  - Source: [van Amsterdam 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003)
- **Cheung et al., Sci Rep, 30 Dec 2024.** Pilot RCT in Hong Kong, n=100 daily smokers who drink, 82% male.
  - Brief alcohol intervention added to cessation clinic care.
  - Validated abstinence at 2 months: 11.8% vs 10.2% (RR 1.15, 0.38–3.53; not significant).
  - Across all participants, a reduction in alcohol-related relapse risk was associated with higher validated abstinence (aOR 1.55, 1.05–2.28).
  - Source: [Cheung 2024, PubMed 39738696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39738696/)

**Does quitting smoking raise drinking, or harm efforts to cut alcohol?**
- **ITC 4-country cohort.** Quitting smoking "in and of itself, does not lead to meaningful changes in alcohol use". Sustained quitters did not cut drinking more than continuing smokers. This was consistent across countries and sexes — [Kahler 2010 ITC, PubMed 20227840](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20227840/)
- **Berg et al., Addict Behav 2015** (Wisconsin cessation-trial participants with daily diaries). "For most smokers alcohol use decreased following smoking cessation." Those who drank more before quitting were more likely to drink more afterwards, and the authors flag them for attention — [Berg 2015, PubMed 25997014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25997014/)
- **Prochaska, Delucchi & Hall, J Consult Clin Psychol, Dec 2004.** Meta-analysis of 19 RCTs in people in addiction treatment or recovery.
  - Smoking cessation interventions during addiction treatment were associated with a 25% increase in long-term abstinence from alcohol and drugs.
  - Their conclusion: they "appeared to enhance rather than compromise long-term sobriety".
  - The smoking effects themselves were not significant at long-term follow-up.
  - Source: [Prochaska 2004, PubMed 15612860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15612860/)
- **Apollonio, Philipps & Bero, Cochrane, Nov 2016; 35 RCTs, n=5,796.** People in treatment for, or recovering from, substance use disorders.
  - Tobacco treatment increased tobacco abstinence: medication RR 1.60, counselling plus medication RR 1.74, and in alcohol dependence RR 1.47 (1.20–1.81).
  - It was "not associated with a difference in abstinence rates from alcohol and other drugs" (RR 0.97, 0.91–1.03; 11 studies).
  - Source: [Apollonio 2016, PubMed 27878808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27878808/)
- **Weinberger et al., J Clin Psychiatry, Feb 2017.** US national NESARC data, n=5,515 adults with remitted substance use disorders.
  - Continued smoking was associated with relapse to a substance use disorder (aOR 2.02, 1.65–2.47) compared with not smoking.
  - Taking up smoking was associated with aOR 4.86 for relapse.
  - Source: [Weinberger 2017, DOI 10.4088/JCP.15m10062](https://doi.org/10.4088/JCP.15m10062)
- **Doing both at once vs one after the other.** Some alcohol-dependent people may prefer to tackle them in sequence because doing both at once may hurt alcohol outcomes. The authors call this literature "not conclusive" (Kodl 2006). One study found lower alcohol abstinence with concurrent treatment among Caucasian but not African American participants (Fu 2008) — [van Amsterdam 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003)
- **Brief alcohol interventions in current smokers (Gerbracht et al., Eur Addict Res, Apr 2025).** In a German RCT of a computer-based brief alcohol intervention, the intervention did not reduce heavy-drinking days in people who currently smoked daily (IRR 1.09, 0.98–1.22). Light-touch alcohol advice alone may be weaker in smokers — [Gerbracht 2025, PubMed 40228485](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40228485/)

**Guidance on alcohol in the first weeks**
- **smokefree.gov (US National Cancer Institute).**
  - "It's a good idea to try to stay away from alcohol in the first few weeks of quitting."
  - Drinking can make the quit harder even after that, so avoid binge drinking. If drinking, stay moderate (up to 1 drink a day for women, 2 for men).
  - Alcohol makes you "more likely to act without thinking". Drinking settings often include more smokers, so plan ahead and drink with people who support the quit.
  - Source: [smokefree.gov – Alcohol & Smoking](https://smokefree.gov/challenges-when-quitting/cravings-triggers/alcohol-smoking)
- **New York State Smokers' Quitline (Roswell Park).** Suggests cutting back or choosing non-alcoholic drinks "until you become a confident nonsmoker". It also suggests avoiding places that serve alcohol for at least the first few weeks, rehearsing refusals, and planning what to do when others go out to smoke. This was read from a search summary, not fetched in full — [NYS Quitline – Alcohol](https://quitweb.roswellpark.org/StayQuit/Alcohol)
- **NHS "10 self-help tips to stop smoking".** The page I fetched did not mention alcohol — [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/quit-smoking/10-self-help-tips-to-stop-smoking/)

### Inferences
- **A full stop on alcohol in weeks 1–2 is well supported.** Week 2 abstinence is the strongest predictor of long-term success (see section 5), and alcohol multiplies the risk of a lapse by about 4–8 times in heavy drinkers. Kahler 2008 found the benefit of the alcohol intervention concentrated in the first 2 weeks after quitting.
- **Wine at home and beer at the golf course carry different risks.** In Kahler 2010, alcohol-involved lapses happened overwhelmingly in social and bar settings with friends and other smokers. Only 23% happened at home. An occasional glass of wine at home with a non-smoking wife from week 3 is a lower-risk context than golf-course beer with smoking friends.
- **The evidence argues for keeping the golf course alcohol-free for longer than 2 weeks.** Bring non-alcoholic drinks and have a plan for when others light up. Smokefree.gov's "first few weeks" and the quitline's "until you become a confident nonsmoker" both support a longer gap. Even moderate drinking raised lapse hazard about 4-fold in heavy drinkers.
- **Changing both at once looks favourable rather than harmful.** Quitting smoking does not appear to push up drinking. Integrated alcohol-and-smoking support improved smoking outcomes in two of the larger RCTs. The main caveat, possible harm to alcohol outcomes from concurrent treatment, comes from people with alcohol dependence, not social heavy drinkers. If he has alcohol dependence (e.g. withdrawal symptoms), medical advice is needed. I did not research alcohol withdrawal here.

### Gaps
- **The "2016 JAMA Psychiatry / Addiction" study the brief mentioned** (showing quitting smoking does not raise alcohol use) was not identified. Closest equivalents found: Kahler 2010 (Drug Alcohol Depend), Berg 2015 (Addict Behav), Apollonio 2016 (Cochrane), Weinberger 2017 (J Clin Psychiatry).
- **Shiffman studies.** I could not get a direct figure from Shiffman et al. 1996 for the share of relapses involving drinking. The "about one quarter of initial lapses" figure is as cited in Kahler 2010.
- **Guidance from NCSCT, NHS and CDC.** I could not find specific advice on alcohol in the first weeks of a quit attempt. The CDC Tips page was not retrieved, and the NHS self-help page does not mention alcohol.
- **No RCT has tested "zero alcohol for 2 weeks, then light drinking"** against other alcohol schedules during a quit attempt.

## 5. Very brief advice, the "not a puff" rule, and lapses predicting relapse

### Takeaway
Brief advice from a doctor raises quit rates by about 66% relative to none (RR 1.66), roughly 1–3 extra quitters per 100. Offering help rather than just advising generates more quit attempts. The NCSCT "not a puff" rule (2025 briefing, reviewed March 2026) rests on strong observational evidence that early lapses predict relapse. In Kenford 1994, 83–97% of patch users who smoked at all in week 2 were smoking at 6 months, vs 41–46% abstinence among those who did not. However, the rule has never been tested head-to-head.

### Cited Findings
- **Kenford et al., JAMA, 23 Feb 1994** (two placebo-controlled nicotine patch trials, n=88 and 112).
  - Any smoking in week 2 after quitting was "a consistent and powerful predictor of failure". Among active-patch users who smoked at all in week 2, 83% and 97% were smoking at 6 months.
  - Among those fully abstinent in week 2, 46% and 41% were abstinent at 6 months.
  - Of patch users still smoking at 6 months, 74% had started smoking in week 1 or 2. For placebo users the figure was 86%.
  - Traditional dependence measures did not predict outcome consistently.
  - The authors advise emphasising "total abstinence" and following up within the first 2 weeks.
  - Source: [Kenford 1994, PubMed 8301790](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8301790/)
- **NCSCT Briefing 11, "The 'not a puff' rule"** (Shahab & Kenyon; © 2025; last reviewed March 2026).
  - Lapses are followed by relapse "in up to 95% of cases". A first lapse is quickly followed by a second.
  - In general-population surveys, abrupt cessation is "nearly twice as likely" to lead to long-term abstinence as cutting down. Service users who chose abrupt quitting were nearly twice as likely to be abstinent.
  - Self-efficacy falls after a lapse. The "abstinence violation effect" can turn a slip into full relapse.
  - The 4-week quit definition allows smoking in days 1–14, but this "should NOT be interpreted clinically" as encouraged.
  - Caveat: "there have been no direct comparisons of the 'not a puff' rule with other approaches".
  - Source: [NCSCT not-a-puff briefing (PDF)](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/index.php/library/view/pdf/not-a-puff-rule.pdf)
- **NHS consumer messaging (UK).** If you can stay smoke-free for 28 days, "you're 5 times more likely to quit for good" — [NHS 10 self-help tips](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/quit-smoking/10-self-help-tips-to-stop-smoking/)
- **Hughes, Keely & Naud, Addiction, Jan 2004** (review of relapse curves). In unaided quit attempts, "most relapse occurs in the first 8 days". Only 3–5% of self-quitters reach 6–12 months of continuous abstinence after a given attempt — [Hughes 2004, PubMed 14678060](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14678060/)
- **Kirchner, Shiffman & Wileyto, J Abnorm Psychol 2011** (EMA study, 203 abstainers, 1,001 lapses).
  - Responses to the first lapse (self-blame, self-efficacy, guilt) did not predict relapse.
  - With repeated lapses, a drop in self-efficacy sped up the next lapse (HR 1.09).
  - Nicotine patch slowed lapse progression (HR 0.58, 0.48–0.70), but this effect faded over multiple lapses.
  - Source: [Kirchner 2011, PubMed 21787035](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21787035/)
- **Physician brief advice (Stead et al., Cochrane, May 2013; 42 trials, more than 31,000 smokers).**
  - Brief advice vs none: RR 1.66 (1.42–1.94). Intensive vs minimal advice: RR 1.37.
  - With an unassisted quit rate of 2–3%, brief advice adds about 1–3 percentage points.
  - Source: [Stead 2013, PubMed 23728631](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23728631/)
- **Aveyard et al., Addiction, 2012** (13 studies; the basis for "very brief advice"). Advice on medical grounds vs nothing increased quit attempts (RR 1.24). Offering behavioural support (RR 2.17) or NRT (RR 1.68) did better, and offering help beat advice alone in direct comparisons (RR 1.69 for behavioural support, RR 1.39 for medication) — [Aveyard 2012, PubMed 22175545](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22175545/)

### Inferences
- For his first 2 weeks, the evidence points to treating any puff as a serious warning sign. It does not mean failure (Kirchner 2011 found the first-lapse reaction did not predict relapse). Weeks 1–2 are the highest-risk window, and they coincide with his alcohol-free fortnight.
- If he slips, the practical message is to get straight back to zero rather than "re-quitting next Monday". The medication (patch) slows the slide from lapse to relapse.
- A practitioner follow-up contact within 14 days of 26 Oct (i.e., by about 9 Nov 2026) matches the Kenford recommendation.

### Gaps
- No RCT directly tests the "not a puff" instruction. The 95% lapse-to-relapse figure comes from older observational studies cited in the NCSCT briefing, which I did not check individually.

## 6. Relapsed smokers: what makes repeat attempts succeed, and how many attempts does it take?

### Takeaway
Relapse is the norm. A longitudinal Canadian cohort estimated an average of about 30 attempts (range of estimates 6–142) before success, and unaided attempts succeed only 3–5% of the time. Past attempts predict trying again, but not succeeding. Only dependence consistently predicts success. The changeable factors are using proven treatment on each attempt (medication plus support, which roughly doubles quit rates over minimal care), avoiding early lapses, and managing triggers such as alcohol. Behavioural relapse-prevention programmes added after quitting do not help (RR 0.98). Extended varenicline does (RR 1.23).

### Cited Findings
- **Chaiton et al., BMJ Open, Jun 2016** (Ontario Tobacco Survey, n=1,277 smokers who made a quit attempt, followed up to 3 years). The estimated average number of attempts before success:
  - 6.1 using older assumptions
  - 19.6 with a constant rate
  - 29.6 with the least-biased method
  - 142 including recalled history
  - The authors: "it may take 30 or more quit attempts". Source: [Chaiton 2016, PubMed 27288378](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27288378/)
- **Vangeli et al., Addiction, 2011** (systematic review of general-population studies). "Past quit attempts and measures of motivation to stop are highly predictive of quit attempts, whereas only measures of dependence are consistently predictive of success". Age, sex, marital status and education were not consistent predictors — [Vangeli 2011, PubMed 21752135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21752135/)
- **Unaided attempts.** 3–5% of self-quitters achieve 6–12 months of abstinence, and most relapse happens in the first 8 days — [Hughes 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14678060/)
- **Population quit rates (as cited in van Amsterdam 2023).** About two-thirds of smokers want to quit and more than half try each year, but only about 6% succeed, and fewer than 10% stay abstinent for 6–12 months — [van Amsterdam 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003)
- **Combined medication and behavioural support** vs usual care or brief advice: RR 1.83 (1.68–1.98) — [Stead 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27009521/)
- **Relapse prevention (Livingstone-Banks et al., Cochrane, 28 Oct 2019; 81 studies, n=69,094).**
  - Behavioural relapse-prevention interventions in people who had quit with help gave "no worthwhile benefit": RR 0.98 (0.87–1.11; 11 studies, n=5,523; moderate certainty). In unaided quitters: RR 1.06 (0.96–1.16).
  - Extended varenicline: RR 1.23 (1.08–1.41; moderate). NRT in unaided quitters: RR 1.24 (1.04–1.47).
  - Source: [Livingstone-Banks 2019, PubMed 31684681](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31684681/)
- **Heavy drinking more than once a week** marks especially low quit rates — [Kahler 2010 ITC](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20227840/). In the Toll 2015 trial, no heavy drinking was associated with OR 1.87 for quitting — [Toll 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25419583/)

### Inferences
- **A past relapse does not make the next attempt less likely to succeed.** Prior attempts are not a negative predictor of success (Vangeli). Framing the past relapse as normal, not as failure, is consistent with the evidence.
- **Changes from the previous attempt that the evidence supports:**
  - use medication plus structured support on every attempt
  - remove the alcohol trigger in the early weeks
  - be strict about not smoking at all in weeks 1–2
  - if he relapsed late last time, consider asking a prescriber about longer medication courses
- **The recall-based figure of about 30 attempts is an average across all smokers.** It mixes unaided attempts (3–5% success) with treated ones. With treatment, each attempt has a higher chance of success.

### Gaps
- I found no recent (2023–2026) study specifically on what predicts success in people who relapse and try again, beyond the general-population predictor reviews.
- Data on recency of the last attempt, or on what caused the previous relapse, as predictors for this individual were not researched.

## 7. What is genuinely new in 2024–2026?

### Takeaway
The main changes since 2023:
- The Cochrane incentives review was updated in Jan 2025, with its effect estimate unchanged (RR 1.52), the evidence that effects outlast payment strengthened, and pregnancy evidence upgraded to high certainty.
- The first fully powered AI chatbot trial (QuitBot) showed no advantage over text messaging (registry results, May 2026).
- App evidence strengthened (2026 meta-analysis; a large German Smoke Free app RCT preprint).
- A 2023 systematic review consolidated the case for stopping or cutting alcohol during a quit attempt.

The core behavioural evidence (Cochrane 2016–2021) has not been superseded.

### Cited Findings
- **Jan 2025 – Cochrane incentives update.**
  - 48 studies, 15 of them new. RR 1.52 (1.33–1.74), high certainty.
  - The effect is sustained after incentives end (RR 1.46). No dose-response with incentive value. Self-deposits were included.
  - Pregnancy RR 2.13, upgraded to high certainty.
  - Source: [Notley 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39799985/)
- **Jul 2024 – QuitBot pilot.** Promising subgroup results — [Bricker 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38913882/)
- **Oct 2024 – QuitBot full-trial protocol.** A chatbot based on a large language model — [protocol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39490766/)
- **May 2026 – QuitBot registry results.** Null at 12 months vs SmokefreeTXT (OR 0.96, 0.76–1.21) — [NCT04308759](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04308759)
- **Jul 2026 – AI review preprint.** No RCTs of large-language-model interventions for smoking or alcohol up to Jan 2025, and no strong evidence that AI assistance adds benefit — [Harrison 2026 preprint](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10117247/v1)
- **Jul 2026 – BMJ EBM app meta-analysis.** Apps vs minimal support RR 2.85 (low certainty). Process-based (e.g. ACT) apps beat traditional apps (RR 1.36, high certainty) — [Chu 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41529951/)
- **Mar 2026 – RAUCHFREI preprint.** Smoke Free app, 39.3% vs 24.4% at 6 months (OR 2.01). Funded by the developer and self-reported — [Keller 2026 preprint](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.03.17.26348617)
- **Jan 2024 – e-health meta-analysis.** Sustained quitting RR 1.79 vs traditional interventions — [Li 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38273889/)
- **Jun 2023 – van Amsterdam systematic review.** Recommends stopping or substantially reducing alcohol during a quit attempt — [van Amsterdam 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.alcohol.2022.12.003)
- **Dec 2024 – Cheung pilot RCT** of a brief alcohol intervention in a smoking clinic. Not significant, but lower alcohol-related relapse risk tracked with abstinence — [Cheung 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39738696/)
- **Apr 2025 – Gerbracht RCT.** A computer brief alcohol intervention did not cut heavy drinking in daily smokers — [Gerbracht 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40228485/)
- **2025 / Mar 2026 – NCSCT "not a puff" briefing** re-issued (© 2025) and reviewed (Mar 2026). It notes that "cut down to stop" options are emerging, but that abrupt cessation evidence remains strong — [NCSCT](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/index.php/library/view/pdf/not-a-puff-rule.pdf)

### Inferences
- For practical purposes in late 2026, the evidence-based kit has not changed: medication, plus human or quitline support, plus text messages or an ACT-style app, plus optionally a financial stake. The real new data points are that incentives keep working after payment stops, and that AI chatbots have not yet beaten simpler digital tools.

### Gaps
- I found no 2024–2026 Cochrane updates for behavioural counselling, telephone support, mobile phone interventions or relapse prevention.
- The QuitBot and RAUCHFREI results are not yet peer reviewed. Any LLM-coach RCTs published after early 2025 may have been missed.
